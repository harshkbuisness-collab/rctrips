# Day 1: "The Crash That Broke America" (working title)

**Target:** US / tier-1 viewers, ages 20–45, who invest a little or worry about the economy.
**Length:** about 2,150 spoken words, about 14 min at 150 words/min.
**Tone:** calm, dry, slightly sarcastic narrator. Story first, lesson last. A small joke every 20–30 s.
**Tags:** `[calm] [serious] [sarcastic] [dramatic] [excited]` set the voice. Everything in *[VISUAL: …]* is a note for the video builder, not spoken.

**Title options (pick one):**
1. The Crash That Broke America
2. 1929: The Day Everyone Got Rich… Until Tuesday
3. You'd Have Waited 25 Years to Break Even

**Thumbnail statement:** "Nobody Saw It Coming." Show a real 1929 Dow chart with a red line falling off a cliff, and a small worried narrator bottom-left.

**Facts to re-check before recording** (all are standard textbook facts; sources are listed at the end):
- Dow peak 381.17 (3 Sep 1929); low 41.22 (8 Jul 1932), −89%; back above 381 only on 23 Nov 1954.
- Black Thursday 24 Oct 1929; Black Monday 28 Oct (−12.8%); Black Tuesday 29 Oct (−11.7%, about 16.4M shares).
- Margin buying with as little as 10% down; brokers' loans about $8.5B in 1929.
- Model T price fell from about $850 (1908) to $260 (1925).
- Irving Fisher, "permanently high plateau" (Oct 1929). Roger Babson, "sooner or later a crash is coming" (5 Sep 1929).
- Hoover, "nearer to the final triumph over poverty than ever before" (Aug 1928).
- Bank of United States closed 11 Dec 1930 (about 400,000 depositors). About 9,000 banks failed in the 1930s. Money supply fell nearly 30%, fall 1930 to winter 1933 (Federal Reserve History).
- Smoot-Hawley Tariff signed June 1930; 1,028 economists asked Hoover to veto it. World trade fell by about two-thirds, 1929–34.
- Unemployment about 25% (1933). Bonus Army, July 1932.
- FDR inaugurated 4 Mar 1933, bank holiday 6 Mar; FDIC 1933; SEC 1934; Social Security 1935. FDIC limit today: $250,000.

## COLD OPEN (0:00–0:50)

[serious] If you put a thousand dollars into the American stock market in September 1929, you would not see your thousand dollars again until 1954.
*[VISUAL: real Dow chart 1925–1955, small narrator at bottom right; red handwritten "25 YEARS" with an arrow across the gap]*

Twenty-five years.
*[VISUAL: close-up of the narrator's head, dead-inside face]*

And that's not because you picked a bad stock. You bought *the whole market*. The safest bet in America.

[dramatic] In the space of three years, the market lost almost ninety percent of its value. One in four workers lost their job. Nine thousand banks shut their doors, and the people's savings went with them.
*[VISUAL: the scene builds up: stock line falling → crowd of small figures → row of bank icons closing one by one, each with a red X]*

[calm] But here's the part they skip in school. The crash didn't cause the Great Depression on its own. It was the *spark*. What turned a bad week on Wall Street into the worst ten years in American history was a chain of very human decisions. And a few of those decisions sound… uncomfortably familiar.
*[VISUAL: newspaper prop with the headline "STOCKS CRASH"; narrator suspicious]*

So let's go back to the party, before anyone knew it was ending.
*[VISUAL: small red section title "The party" top-left]*

## CHAPTER 1: THE PARTY (0:50–3:00)

[calm] It's 1922. The war is over, America won, and everyone has decided that the future is going to be *amazing*.
*[VISUAL: title "The party"; narrator in a flat cap waving; small skyline]*

And honestly, it was. For the first time, ordinary families could buy things that felt like science fiction. Electric refrigerators. Vacuum cleaners. And the radio, which was basically the iPhone of the 1920s. Everybody had to have one, and nobody fully understood how it worked.
*[VISUAL: icons appear one by one around a happy family: fridge, vacuum, radio; label "the iPhone of 1925"]*

Then there was Henry Ford. His moving assembly line took the price of a Model T from about eight hundred and fifty dollars down to two hundred and sixty. A car went from a rich man's toy to something a factory worker could actually own.
*[VISUAL: price tag props $850 → $260 with a red arrow down; small car; assembly-line drawing]*

[sarcastic] But there was a small problem. Most families still didn't have two hundred and sixty dollars lying around. So America invented a brand new idea… buy now, pay later.
*[VISUAL: narrator smug; sign prop "BUY NOW, PAY LATER"]*

The installment plan. A little down, a little every month. Radios on credit, furniture on credit, cars on credit. By the late twenties, most cars in America were bought on credit.
*[VISUAL: a row of small items, each with a tiny credit-card tag]*

[calm] And it worked, for a while. Factories made more, workers earned more, people bought more. Prices were stable, wages were going up, and the economy felt like a machine that only had one gear: forward.
*[VISUAL: simple machine diagram with arrows: factories → wages → shopping → factories]*

But underneath the party, two groups of people weren't invited.
*[VISUAL: party scene on the left; two small sad groups on the right]*

The first was farmers. During the war, Europe needed American food, so farmers borrowed money to buy more land and more tractors. When the war ended, Europe started growing its own food again, prices collapsed, and farmers were stuck with the debt. A quarter of Americans still lived on farms. That's a lot of people quietly going broke during the "Roaring" Twenties.
*[VISUAL: farmer character with a tractor and a debt-monster prop; label "25% of Americans"]*

The second group was everyone who looked closely at the numbers. Because the money wasn't just going into radios and Fords anymore. It was going somewhere much more exciting.
*[VISUAL: dollar bills flying toward a stock-exchange building]*

## CHAPTER 2: THE CASINO (3:00–5:30)

*[VISUAL: red title "The casino"]*

[calm] Before the 1920s, the stock market was mostly for rich people in top hats. By 1929, it was for everyone.
*[VISUAL: top-hat banker character, then a crowd of small regular people pushing in]*

[sarcastic] Teachers, nurses, factory workers, your barber. There's a famous story, maybe true, maybe not, that a rich investor named Joseph Kennedy got a stock tip from the boy shining his shoes… and decided it was time to sell everything. When the shoeshine boy is giving stock tips, the party might be ending.
*[VISUAL: shoeshine scene; speech bubble "Buy RCA, mister!"; narrator side-eye]*

And here's the thing that made it truly dangerous. You didn't need much money to play. You could buy stocks *on margin*.
*[VISUAL: label "margin" in red handwriting]*

[serious] Here's how margin worked. You want a hundred dollars of stock. You put down ten dollars of your own money, and your broker lends you the other ninety. If the stock goes up ten percent, you've made ten dollars on a ten-dollar bet. You just doubled your money.
*[VISUAL: bar: $10 yours (green) + $90 borrowed (grey); stock +10% → "+100% for you!"]*

[dramatic] But if the stock drops ten percent, your ten dollars is gone. And if it drops more, you owe the broker money you don't have.
*[VISUAL: same bar, red; "−100%"; narrator panicking]*

By 1929, Americans had borrowed around eight and a half *billion* dollars just to buy stocks. For context, that was more than all the cash circulating in the entire country at the time.
*[VISUAL: two stacks of money: "loans to buy stocks" vs "all US cash"; red circle around the bigger one]*

[sarcastic] Wall Street had its own tricks too. Groups of wealthy traders formed "pools". They'd quietly buy a stock, trade it back and forth among themselves to make it look popular, let the newspapers hype it up… then sell to everyone who came running. Totally illegal today. Totally normal back then.
*[VISUAL: three smug figures passing one stock certificate in a circle; crowd running in; label "pump… and dump"]*

[calm] And the market went up. Then up again. From 1921 to its peak in September 1929, the Dow Jones went up about six times. People started believing the line could only go one way.
*[VISUAL: line chart building up step by step; small character riding the line like a rollercoaster]*

Even the experts agreed. One of the most famous economists in America, a Yale professor named Irving Fisher, said stock prices had reached… quote… "a permanently high plateau."
*[VISUAL: professor figure at a podium; quote box with the words underlined in yellow]*

[sarcastic] He said that about two weeks before the crash. Not his best work.
*[VISUAL: close-up of the narrator, cringing]*

There *were* warnings. In September, an investor named Roger Babson stood up and said, "Sooner or later a crash is coming, and it may be terrific." That day, the market dropped. People called it the "Babson Break"… and then they went right back to buying.
*[VISUAL: Babson figure with a warning sign; crowd covering their ears; "Babson Break" label]*

[calm] Even the new president, Herbert Hoover, had promised America was "nearer to the final triumph over poverty than ever before in the history of any land."
*[VISUAL: politician at podium, speech bubble; tiny calendar "1928"]*

Everyone was a genius. Everyone was rich, on paper. And almost nobody asked what would happen if the line went down.

## CHAPTER 3: THE CRASH (5:30–7:15)

*[VISUAL: red title "The crash"; no sting music, a short pause instead]*

[serious] Thursday, October 24th, 1929.
*[VISUAL: calendar prop "THU OCT 24, 1929"]*

The market opened, and the selling started. And then it didn't stop. Prices fell so fast that the ticker tape, the machine that printed stock prices, fell more than an hour behind. People were selling stocks without knowing what the price even was.
*[VISUAL: ticker-tape machine drawing with a clock; label "1 hour behind"; traders' heads with question marks]*

[calm] Crowds gathered outside the New York Stock Exchange. Police were sent to Wall Street.
*[VISUAL: crowd of small figures outside the exchange building; police figure]*

Around noon, the biggest bankers in New York met in secret and decided to rescue the market. They sent one man onto the trading floor, Richard Whitney, the vice president of the exchange. He walked up and loudly offered to buy U.S. Steel way above the market price. Then he did the same with stock after stock.
*[VISUAL: banker character walking through a crowd, big speech bubble "U.S. Steel, 205!"]*

[sarcastic] And it worked! Prices bounced. The newspapers relaxed. Crisis over.
*[VISUAL: thumbs-up narrator; newspaper "Bankers halt panic"]*

[dramatic] For about three days.
*[VISUAL: hard cut; narrator's face drops]*

On Monday, the Dow fell almost thirteen percent. On Tuesday, October 29th, it fell almost twelve percent more. Sixteen million shares changed hands, a record that stood for decades. And this time, nobody came to the rescue.
*[VISUAL: the chart cliff; "−13%", "−12%" in red marker; small figures falling off the line]*

[serious] Remember margin? This is where it turned deadly. As prices dropped, brokers called their customers and demanded more money: "margin calls". People who didn't have the money had their stocks sold automatically, which pushed prices down further, which triggered more margin calls.
*[VISUAL: loop diagram drawn arrow by arrow: price falls → margin call → forced selling → price falls]*

In a few weeks, the market lost about forty percent. Paper fortunes that took years to build disappeared before lunch.

[calm] But here's the strange part. By the spring of 1930, the market had actually recovered some of its losses. A lot of people thought the worst was over.
*[VISUAL: small bounce on the chart; narrator relieved]*

It wasn't. The worst hadn't even started.

## CHAPTER 4: THE REAL DISASTER (7:15–9:40)

*[VISUAL: red title "The banks"]*

[serious] The crash hurt investors. What happened next hurt *everyone*. And it started with the banks.
*[VISUAL: bank-building character, nervous]*

In 1930, there was no such thing as deposit insurance. If your bank failed, your savings were simply… gone. No government guarantee. No phone number to call.
*[VISUAL: savings book prop with a red X; label "no insurance"]*

And a lot of small banks had done exactly what everyone else did. They'd lent money to people buying stocks, or invested in stocks themselves. When the market crashed, those loans went bad.
*[VISUAL: bank → arrow to stock chart → red arrow down]*

So people did the only logical thing. They ran. If you hear your bank might be in trouble, you don't wait to find out. You go get your money *right now*. And when everybody does that at once, even a healthy bank collapses, because banks don't keep all your money in the building. It's lent out.
*[VISUAL: crowd running to a bank door; inside, a small vault with only a few coins; label "most of it is lent out"]*

[dramatic] In December 1930, a rumor spread in New York about a bank with a very official-sounding name: the Bank of United States. Thousands of people lined up to pull their money out. Within days, the bank closed. Around four hundred thousand depositors were locked out of their savings.
*[VISUAL: long queue of small figures; bank sign "BANK OF UNITED STATES"; door slams; "400,000 people"]*

[sarcastic] It wasn't even a government bank. It just sounded like one, which made the panic so much worse.

[serious] Then it spread. Wave after wave of bank runs, city after city. Over the next few years, about nine thousand American banks failed.
*[VISUAL: map of the US filling with small bank icons turning red one by one]*

[calm] And here's where it turned from bad to catastrophic. When banks fail and people hide cash under the mattress, the amount of money moving through the economy shrinks. Between 1930 and 1933, America's money supply fell by nearly thirty percent.
*[VISUAL: pile of money shrinking by a third; mattress with cash under it]*

Less money means prices fall. That sounds nice… cheaper groceries! But think about it from the other side. If you're a farmer, your crops sell for less, but your mortgage payment stays exactly the same. If you're a factory, your products sell for less, so you cut wages, or you cut workers. Every falling price is somebody's falling paycheck.
*[VISUAL: price tags going down; farmer with an unchanged mortgage bill "$500/month" in red; factory with workers being removed one at a time]*

[serious] And the Federal Reserve, the one institution built to stop exactly this kind of panic, mostly stood back and watched. Decades later, a future Fed chairman, Ben Bernanke, publicly admitted it: "Regarding the Great Depression… we did it. We're very sorry."
*[VISUAL: Fed building; quote box with yellow highlight on "we did it"]*

## CHAPTER 5: HOW TO MAKE IT WORSE (9:40–10:50)

*[VISUAL: red title "Making it worse"]*

[sarcastic] Washington also had some ideas. Unfortunately.
*[VISUAL: Capitol building; narrator facepalm]*

In June 1930, Congress passed the Smoot-Hawley Tariff, big new taxes on imported goods, to protect American farmers and factories. More than a thousand economists signed a letter begging President Hoover not to sign it.
*[VISUAL: stack of paper with "1,028 signatures"; politician ignoring it]*

He signed it anyway.

[calm] Other countries did exactly what you'd expect. They put their own tariffs on American goods. World trade fell by about two-thirds over the next few years. American farmers, the people the tariff was supposed to help, lost the customers they had left.
*[VISUAL: two maps throwing tariff walls at each other; trade arrows shrinking]*

Then, in 1932, with the economy already on the floor, the government raised taxes sharply to balance the budget. Squeezing an economy that was already struggling to breathe.
*[VISUAL: hand squeezing a tiny economy character]*

## CHAPTER 6: THE BOTTOM (10:50–12:20)

*[VISUAL: red title "The bottom"; a real 1930s photo (public domain, Library of Congress), slow fade-in, no zoom]*

[serious] By 1932, the stock market had lost eighty-nine percent of its value from the peak. A share that was worth a hundred dollars was now worth about eleven.
*[VISUAL: price tag "$100" crossed out → "$11"]*

[calm] By 1933, one in four American workers had no job. There was no unemployment insurance. No food stamps. Just lines. Breadlines stretching around city blocks. Men in suits, men who used to be bankers and salesmen, waiting for a bowl of soup.
*[VISUAL: long breadline of small figures in hats; one with a briefcase]*

Families who lost their homes built shacks out of scrap wood and cardboard. People called them "Hoovervilles".
*[VISUAL: shack town drawing; label "Hooverville"; small politician looking away]*

[dramatic] And then nature joined in. In the Great Plains, years of drought and over-plowed soil turned the farmland to dust. Storms of black dust rolled across Oklahoma, Kansas and Texas, so thick they blocked out the sun. Millions of people left the Plains, many heading west to California, looking for any kind of work.
*[VISUAL: storm cloud prop, dust wall; car loaded with furniture driving west; real Dust Bowl photo (public domain)]*

[serious] In the summer of 1932, around twenty thousand World War I veterans marched on Washington. They'd been promised a bonus for their service, payable in 1945. They needed it now. They camped near the Capitol, peacefully, for weeks.
*[VISUAL: tent camp with veterans; sign "PAY THE BONUS"]*

In the end, the army was sent in to clear them out. Soldiers, using tear gas, against the soldiers of the last war. Americans saw the photos, and something broke.
*[VISUAL: a real 1932 Bonus Army newspaper or photo (public domain); narrator silent, close-up]*

## CHAPTER 7: THE WAY BACK (12:20–13:30)

*[VISUAL: red title "The way back"]*

[calm] In March 1933, Franklin Roosevelt took office and said the most famous line of the whole era: "The only thing we have to fear is fear itself."
*[VISUAL: politician at podium; quote box]*

Two days later, he closed every bank in the country. A "bank holiday". Inspectors checked the books, and only the healthy banks were allowed to reopen. Then he went on the radio and explained, in plain English, why your money would be safer in the bank than under your mattress.
*[VISUAL: bank with a "CLOSED" sign → "OPEN" sign; family around a radio]*

[sarcastic] And people believed him, which tells you how good that radio voice must have been.

[calm] When the banks reopened, people actually *put money back in*. The bank runs stopped.
*[VISUAL: line of people now depositing; green arrow]*

Then came the rules we still live with today. The FDIC, so your bank deposits are insured. Today that's up to two hundred and fifty thousand dollars. The SEC, so companies have to tell the truth to investors, and those "pools" from earlier became illegal. And Social Security.
*[VISUAL: three props appear one by one with labels: shield "FDIC $250K", document "SEC", envelope "Social Security"]*

The recovery was slow and bumpy. It took World War Two to finally bring unemployment all the way down. And the stock market? It didn't get back to its 1929 peak until November 1954.

## CHAPTER 8: SO, COULD IT HAPPEN AGAIN? (13:30–14:30)

*[VISUAL: red title "Could it happen again?"]*

[calm] A lot of what broke in 1929 has been fixed. Your bank deposits are insured. The Fed now steps in fast instead of watching. Companies have to report their numbers.
*[VISUAL: three green check marks next to the three props]*

[serious] But some things can't be fixed with a law. Because they aren't in the banks… they're in us.
*[VISUAL: narrator pointing at his own head]*

Borrowed money that feels like free money. Stock tips from people who've never had a bad year. And the most expensive belief in finance: "this time is different."
*[VISUAL: three red-circled items, written by hand: "borrowed money", "everyone's a genius", "this time is different"]*

[dramatic] Every bubble in history has had all three.
*[VISUAL: wide shot: all props from the video, small, gathered around the narrator]*

[calm] So the next time everyone around you is getting rich on something they can't explain… remember the guy who bought in September 1929. Not because he was stupid. Because he was *sure*.
*[VISUAL: the real Dow chart from the opening again, with "25 YEARS" in red]*

In the next video, we're looking at the other side of that story: who America actually owes all its debt to. The answer might surprise you. Subscribe so you don't miss it.
*[VISUAL: end screen, 10 s]*
