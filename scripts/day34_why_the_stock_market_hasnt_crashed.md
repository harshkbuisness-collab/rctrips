# Day 34: Why the Stock Market Hasn't Crashed Yet
TITLES: Why the Stock Market Hasn't Crashed Yet | Everything Says Crash. So Why Is It Still Going Up? | The Market Should've Crashed. Here's Why It Didn't.
THUMBNAIL: US STOCKS — narrator holding a chart that keeps climbing while warning signs fall around him
THREAD: If everything says crash, who keeps buying?

## CHAPTER 1: The chart nobody can explain
[calm] Here's a strange fact about the American stock market. The S and P five hundred is five hundred companies. But just twenty of them now make up nearly half of its total value.
> VISUAL: G11; D13; A15; "TOP 20"; E49
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, S&P 500 | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%26P_500 | highlight "nearly half of the index's total market cap"
Twenty out of five hundred. That's like a school where twenty kids carry everyone else's backpacks, and the other four hundred eighty just walk behind them.
> VISUAL: C28; E06; A04; "20 vs 480"
And for years, people have been yelling that a crash is coming. High interest rates. Record government debt. Tariffs. Wars. Houses nobody can afford.
> VISUAL: E44; E09; D28; D11; A09
Every one of those sounds like a great reason for stocks to fall. And the market kept hitting new records anyway, which is very rude to everyone who predicted a crash.
> VISUAL: D13; E45; A13; G02; "NEW RECORD"
So everybody ends up asking the same question. If everything says crash, why hasn't it crashed?
> VISUAL: A06; E48; G11; D14; "WHY?"
But the better question is a lot simpler. It's not why the market went up. It's who keeps buying.
> VISUAL: A17; E10; E02; C28; "WHO KEEPS BUYING?"
Because every time a stock goes up, somebody paid a higher price for it. And most of the money doing that buying today never reads the news at all.
> VISUAL: E01; E09; A05; D13; "NEVER READS THE NEWS"
Before we get to who that is, let's look at the two easy answers people usually give. Both are only half right.
> VISUAL: A38; E10; E22; "2 EASY ANSWERS"

## CHAPTER 2: Easy answer one, the economy is fine
[calm] Easy answer number one. The market is up because the economy is fine.
> VISUAL: A21; G13; D13; E33
That's partly true. Over its history, the S and P five hundred has gone up in about seven out of every ten years.
> VISUAL: D13; E45; A02; "7 out of 10"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, S&P 500 | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%26P_500 | highlight "it has posted annual increases 70% of the time"
But a stock is not a report card on how people feel today. A stock is a small slice of a company's future profits.
> VISUAL: A38; E10; D06; E25; "FUTURE PROFITS"
When you buy one, you're paying for money that company might make in five, ten or twenty years.
> VISUAL: E25; E01; E23; A06
So a terrible month at the grocery store doesn't automatically push stock prices down. The market can feel great while your wallet feels awful.
> VISUAL: E37; D02; D13; A35; "WALLET vs MARKET"
That explains a little. But it doesn't explain why prices kept rising through warning after warning. So, easy answer number two.
> VISUAL: E44; D13; A06; E48

## CHAPTER 3: Easy answer two, it's just a few giants
[calm] Easy answer number two. The whole market isn't really going up. Just a handful of giant tech companies are.
> VISUAL: E34; D23; E07; A16; "A FEW GIANTS"
People call them the Magnificent Seven. Microsoft, Apple, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Nvidia and Tesla.
> VISUAL: E34; D23; E07; E08; "MAGNIFICENT 7"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Big Tech | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnificent_Seven_(stocks) | highlight "adds Nvidia and Tesla to the Big Five"
In twenty twenty-three, those seven companies made about two thirds of all the S and P five hundred's gains. Seven companies. Out of five hundred.
> VISUAL: D13; E34; A08; "2/3 OF GAINS"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Big Tech | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnificent_Seven_(stocks) | highlight "accounted for approximately two-thirds of the S&P 500's gains"
That same year, as a group, they returned one hundred seven percent. They more than doubled in twelve months, mostly on excitement about artificial intelligence.
> VISUAL: D23; E26; E45; A36; "107%"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Big Tech | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnificent_Seven_(stocks) | highlight "107% return on investment"
By the middle of twenty twenty-four, they were thirty-one percent of the whole index. So if you put one hundred dollars into an S and P five hundred fund, about thirty-one dollars went to just seven companies.
> VISUAL: E01; E34; D25; A15; "31 of every 100"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Big Tech | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnificent_Seven_(stocks) | highlight "accounted for 31% of the S&P 500"
So yes, a few giants matter a lot. But this answer has a big hole in it.
> VISUAL: E34; E30; A07; E48
Someone still has to buy those giant stocks, every single day, at higher and higher prices. And the answer to who that is, is probably you.
> VISUAL: A01; D25; E01; E45; "YOU"

## CHAPTER 4: The real engine, money on autopilot
[calm] If you have a job with a retirement plan, there's a good chance you were signed up without filling out a single form.
> VISUAL: D25; E06; A28; C09
This is called automatic enrollment. Your employer puts you into the four oh one k, and you have to opt out if you don't want it.
> VISUAL: D25; E10; A38; "AUTO-ENROLL"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, 401(k) | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/401(k) | highlight "Employers are allowed to automatically enroll their employees in 401(k) plans"
A four oh one k, by the way, is a retirement account you get through work. Money goes in before taxes, and it's supposed to stay there until you're old.
> VISUAL: D25; E21; C13; E24
For a long time, many companies were nervous about signing workers up automatically. Then in two thousand six, Congress passed a law that made it a safer choice.
> VISUAL: G09; D24; C05; E14
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, 401(k) | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/401(k) | highlight "The Pension Protection Act of 2006 made automatic enrollment a safer option for employers"
So where does that money go? Very often, into a fund that simply buys the whole market. That's called an index fund.
> VISUAL: D25; E01; G11; A17; "INDEX FUND"
Instead of picking winners, an index fund buys every company in the index, in the same size as the index. Big company, big slice. Small company, small slice.
> VISUAL: E22; E34; E35; A38
The first index fund for regular people started on December thirty-first, nineteen seventy-five. Its founder was a man named John Bogle.
> VISUAL: E25; E09; A27; "1975"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Index fund | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_fund | highlight "Bogle started the First Index Investment Trust on December 31, 1975"
Wall Street hated it. Competitors made fun of the idea of just buying everything and doing nothing. They even called it Bogle's folly, which is a fancy way of saying a dumb idea.
> VISUAL: C02; C23; A13; "BOGLE'S FOLLY"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Index fund | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_fund | highlight "it was heavily derided by competitors"
Bogle predicted his boring little fund would one day be bigger than the most famous stock-picking fund in America. It happened in the year two thousand.
> VISUAL: E28; E45; A04; "2000"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Index fund | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_fund | highlight "which it did in 2000"
And doing nothing kept winning. In twenty twenty-one, seventy-nine percent of professional fund managers did worse than the S and P five hundred. They are paid to beat it, and four out of five didn't.
> VISUAL: C01; D14; G11; A03; "79% LOST"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Passive management | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passive_management | highlight "79% of fund managers underperformed the S&P in 2021"
So more and more money moved into index funds. And here's the key part. An index fund does not care about the news.
> VISUAL: D25; E09; A48; "DOESN'T CARE"
Payday comes, and the fund buys. It buys if the headlines are scary. It buys if prices are already high. It buys every two weeks, no matter what.
> VISUAL: E25; E01; G11; A30; "EVERY PAYDAY"
And because it buys in proportion, more of each paycheck goes to the biggest companies. So the giants get bigger, partly just because they're already big.
> VISUAL: E34; E01; E45; A07
Now multiply that by tens of millions of American workers. That's a river of money flowing into stocks on autopilot, every single payday.
> VISUAL: C28; E01; G11; G13; "AUTOPILOT"
That river is the biggest reason the market didn't crash when the headlines said it should. But it's not the only one.
> VISUAL: D13; E44; A18; E49

## CHAPTER 5: Companies buying themselves
[calm] The second engine is companies buying their own stock. This is called a share buyback.
> VISUAL: E34; E01; G11; A17; "BUYBACK"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Share repurchase | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Share_repurchase | highlight "is the reacquisition by a company of its own shares"
Here's how it works. When a company buys back its shares, there are fewer shares left. So each remaining share owns a bigger piece of the profits.
> VISUAL: A38; E10; D06; "FEWER SHARES"
It's like a pizza with ten slices. If two slices disappear, every slice left is a bigger part of the pizza. Nobody made more pizza. There's just fewer people sharing it.
> VISUAL: E39; E20; A04; "10 SLICES"
In the past, companies mostly shared profits by paying cash to their owners. In the United States, buybacks took off in a big way and never really stopped.
> VISUAL: E01; D13; E34; A27
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Share repurchase | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Share_repurchase | highlight "there was a sharp rise in the volume of share repurchases in the United States"
Almost ninety-five percent of buybacks happen quietly, on the open market, just like any other buyer clicking buy.
> VISUAL: E07; G11; A05; "95% QUIET"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Share repurchase | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Share_repurchase | highlight "representing almost 95% of all repurchases"
They're not always smart, though. Between nineteen ninety-nine and twenty eleven, the computer company HP spent sixty-seven billion dollars buying back its own stock.
> VISUAL: E07; E02; E20; A13; "67 BILLION"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Share repurchase | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Share_repurchase | highlight "HP spent $67 billion on stock buybacks"
Sixty-seven billion dollars. That's a lot of money to spend on buying something you already own. A buyback can lift a stock price without making the company any better.
> VISUAL: E02; E34; A14; E48
So on top of every paycheck, companies themselves are steady buyers of their own stock. Which brings us to engine number three.
> VISUAL: E34; D25; E01; A18

## CHAPTER 6: Cheaper money
[calm] The third engine is interest rates. When rates go down, borrowing gets cheaper, and future profits look more valuable today.
> VISUAL: G04; D14; E01; A38; "RATES DOWN"
Here's a simple way to see it. If a savings account pays five percent, lots of people are happy to just leave money in the bank.
> VISUAL: D15; E04; A25; "5%"
If the bank only pays three percent, that same money starts looking for something better. A lot of it ends up in stocks.
> VISUAL: D15; E01; G11; A33; "3%"
After holding its key rate above five percent for more than a year, the Federal Reserve started cutting in September twenty twenty-four.
> VISUAL: G10; D14; C26; E25
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Federal funds rate | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_funds_rate | highlight "the Federal Reserve began lowering rates in September 2024"
By December twenty twenty-five, the rate was down to about three and a half percent.
> VISUAL: G10; D14; A02; "3.5%"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Federal funds rate | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_funds_rate | highlight "The last cycle of easing monetary policy"
So you have money on autopilot, companies buying themselves, and cheaper money. Three engines, all pushing in the same direction.
> VISUAL: D25; E34; G10; E45; "3 ENGINES"
Does that mean the market can never crash? History has a very clear answer. No. It can, and it has, even when the engines were running.
> VISUAL: E47; D14; A09; "IT CAN"

## CHAPTER 7: Expensive can stay expensive, until it doesn't
[serious] On December fifth, nineteen ninety-six, the head of the Federal Reserve gave a speech on live TV.
> VISUAL: E13; E14; G10; A27
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Irrational exuberance | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrational_exuberance | highlight "televised speech on December 5, 1996"
His name was Alan Greenspan, and he asked whether investors had caught a case of irrational exuberance. In normal English, he was asking if everyone got way too excited.
> VISUAL: E14; C05; A36; "IRRATIONAL EXUBERANCE"
Markets in Tokyo closed down three percent. Everyone thought the top was in.
> VISUAL: G15; D14; A08; "-3%"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Irrational exuberance | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrational_exuberance | highlight "closing off 3%"
It wasn't. The market kept going up for more than three more years. Anyone who sold in nineteen ninety-six missed a huge run.
> VISUAL: D13; E45; A13; "3 MORE YEARS"
The tech-heavy Nasdaq, an index full of new internet companies, rose about six hundred percent between nineteen ninety-five and its peak.
> VISUAL: E07; E26; D13; A36; "600%"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Dot-com bubble | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dot-com_bubble | highlight "rose by 600%"
To measure how expensive stocks are, economists use a number called CAPE. It compares prices to ten years of company profits.
> VISUAL: E10; E22; A38; "CAPE"
The economist Robert Shiller found only three earlier times when it was this high. Around nineteen twenty-nine, nineteen ninety-nine, and two thousand seven.
> VISUAL: E25; E44; A39; "1929 1999 2007"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclically_adjusted_price-to-earnings_ratio | highlight "the years clustered around 1929, 1999 and 2007"
Each one was followed by a big fall. And CAPE hit its all-time record during the dot-com bubble in two thousand.
> VISUAL: D29; E47; D14; A09
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclically_adjusted_price-to-earnings_ratio | highlight "reached an all-time high during the 2000 dot-com bubble"
The Nasdaq peaked on March tenth, two thousand. By October two thousand two, it had fallen seventy-eight percent.
> VISUAL: D14; E46; E07; A42; "-78%"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Dot-com bubble | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dot-com_bubble | highlight "only to fall 78% from its peak by October 2002"
A few people saw it coming. The investor John Templeton bet against internet stocks right at the top. Which is the investing version of leaving the party five minutes before the cops show up.
> VISUAL: C21; D14; A04; "SHORTED THE TOP"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Dot-com bubble | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dot-com_bubble | highlight "successfully shorted many dot-com stocks at the peak of the bubble"
Japan shows the slow version. Its stock market hit an all-time high on December twenty-ninth, nineteen eighty-nine.
> VISUAL: G15; D13; G07; A15; "1989"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Japanese asset price bubble | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_asset_price_bubble | highlight "all-time high of 38,957.44 on December 29, 1989"
And Japan was not some small market. In nineteen eighty-nine, thirty-two of the world's fifty biggest companies were Japanese.
> VISUAL: G15; E34; E43; A08; "32 of 50"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Lost Decades | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_Decades | highlight "In 1989, of the world's top 50 companies by market capitalization , 32 were Japanese"
It didn't beat that high again until February twenty twenty-four. That's thirty-four years of waiting just to get back to even.
> VISUAL: G15; E23; A26; "34 YEARS"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Lost Decades | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_Decades | highlight "In February 2024, Nikkei 225 reached 39,098.68"
And crashes can be fast, too. On one Monday in October nineteen eighty-seven, the Dow fell twenty-two percent in a single day.
> VISUAL: E25; D14; E47; A10; "-22% IN 1 DAY"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Black Monday (1987) | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Monday_(1987) | highlight "the largest one-day drop in the index's history"
Even all that autopilot money didn't stop a fall in twenty twenty-two. The S and P five hundred dropped about a quarter from its high that year.
> VISUAL: D25; D14; G02; A35; "-25%"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, 2022 stock market decline | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_stock_market_decline | highlight "before declining 25% to its low for the year in October 2022"
So high prices don't tell you when a crash comes. Shiller himself said CAPE is not meant to predict crashes. It only says future returns may be lower.
> VISUAL: E10; E48; A14; "NOT A CRASH CLOCK"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclically_adjusted_price-to-earnings_ratio | highlight "It is not intended as an indicator of impending market crashes"

## CHAPTER 8: The real warning signs
[calm] So, back to our question. If everything says crash, who keeps buying? The answer is millions of regular workers, on autopilot, every payday.
> VISUAL: C28; D25; E01; G11; A17
That steady buying is a cushion. It's one reason the market is hard to knock down.
> VISUAL: E21; D13; A21; "CUSHION"
But a market carried by a few giants is fragile in a new way. If those giants stumble, almost everyone's retirement stumbles with them.
> VISUAL: E34; E30; D25; A35
And there's one more quiet change. Many retirement funds slowly move money out of stocks and into bonds as people get closer to retiring.
> VISUAL: D25; D24; E23; C13
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, 401(k) | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/401(k) | highlight "automatically shifting investments from stocks to bonds based on time to planned retirement date"
A bond is a loan you give to a government or a company. It pays you interest, and it usually moves less than stocks.
> VISUAL: D24; G09; E04; A38
So the same autopilot that buys stocks for young workers slowly sells stocks for older workers. And America is getting older.
> VISUAL: C27; C13; E22; A06; "BUY vs SELL"
So if you want to watch for real warning signs, don't watch the scary headlines. They're loud, and they change every day.
> VISUAL: E09; E13; A48; E44
Watch three boring things instead. One. How much of the market sits in just a few companies.
> VISUAL: E10; E34; A38; "1. CONCENTRATION"
Two. How expensive stocks are compared to their profits.
> VISUAL: E10; E22; A38; "2. PRICE vs PROFIT"
Three. Whether the paycheck money keeps flowing in, or people start pulling it out.
> VISUAL: E10; D25; E01; A38; "3. THE PAYCHECK RIVER"
This is not financial advice. It's just how the machine works.
> VISUAL: A04; E22; G11
The market hasn't crashed yet because millions of workers buy every payday, companies buy their own shares, and money got cheaper again.
> VISUAL: C28; E34; G10; D13; A18
None of that makes a crash impossible. It just means the reasons the market goes up are a lot quieter than the reasons people say it should fall.
> VISUAL: D13; E44; A37; "QUIETER"
Next payday, the funds will buy again. And the river keeps flowing, until the day it doesn't.
> VISUAL: E01; G11; E23; A06
Thanks for watching Dollar Diaries.
