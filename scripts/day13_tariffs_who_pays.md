## CHAPTER 2: What a Tariff Really Is
[calm] A tariff is a tax on goods coming into a country. Think of it like a toll on a bridge. Goods have to pay it before they can cross into America.
> VISUAL: A38_narrator_teacher_pointer slides in from the left; G19_tariff_wall drops in; E30_broken_bridge pops in; H17 price tag pops in "TOLL"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tariff | highlight "tariff"
Here's the key part. The foreign factory doesn't pay the toll. The company bringing the goods in does. That's called the importer. In this story, the importer is Dan.
> VISUAL: E33_factory pops in; A48_narrator_stop_hand pops in; H09_guy_curly_neutral walks in from the left; H18 sign drops in "IMPORTER = DAN"
The tax is paid to U.S. Customs and Border Protection, a part of the American government. The money goes to the U.S. Treasury.
> VISUAL: G09_capitol_building drops in; E21_safe_vault pops in; E02_money_bag pops in; red arrow from Dan to the Capitol
> SCREEN: U.S. Customs and Border Protection | https://www.cbp.gov/trade | highlight "Trade"
So on Dan's shipment of five hundred mixers, each costing one hundred dollars, the twenty-five percent tariff means a bill of twelve thousand five hundred dollars.
> VISUAL: H19_box_closed pops in; D27_calculator_shocked drops in; H17 price tag pops in "$12,500"; A35_narrator_sweating slides in from the left
Dan has to pay that before he can get his mixers. Not the factory. Not a foreign government. Dan, with money from his small business account in Indiana.
> VISUAL: H14_bowtie_man_worried walks in from the left; D15_bank_building pops in; E01_cash_stack drops in; G09_capitol_building pops in
So who pays the tariff, at first? The answer is simple. An American business.

But that's only step one. Because what Dan does next decides who really ends up carrying the cost.

## CHAPTER 3: Three Choices
[calm] Now each mixer costs Dan one hundred twenty-five dollars instead of one hundred. He has three choices.
> VISUAL: H17 price tag drops in "$100 → $125"; H16 sticky note pops in "1"; H16 sticky note pops in "2"; H16 sticky note pops in "3"
Choice one: he raises his price, from two hundred dollars to two hundred twenty-five, or more. Then his customers pay the tariff.
> VISUAL: H17 price tag pops in "$200 → $225"; C28_crowd_small drops in; E37_cart_prices_up pops in; H16 sticky note pops in "CUSTOMERS PAY"
Choice two: he keeps his price the same and earns less on every mixer. Then Dan pays the tariff himself, out of his own profit.
> VISUAL: H14_bowtie_man_worried walks in from the left; H17 price tag pops in "$200"; E01_cash_stack pops in; D14_chart_arrow_down draws on
Choice three: he asks the foreign factory to lower its price, to share the cost. Then the foreign company pays part of it.
> VISUAL: E08_phone pops in; E33_factory drops in; H17 price tag pops in "$100 → $95"; E43_globe pops in
That third choice is where the "other countries pay" idea comes from. And sometimes, it does happen a little. But usually not much.
> VISUAL: C05_politician_podium walks in from the right; E22_scale drops in; H16 sticky note pops in "A LITTLE"; A14_narrator_shrug slides in from the left
Why not? Because the factory has other customers around the world. If Dan's price gets too low, the factory can just sell to buyers in Europe or Asia instead.
> VISUAL: E33_factory pops in; E43_globe drops in; G16_map_europe pops in; G17_map_india pops in; red arrow away from America
So who pays depends on who has more power to say no. And in most cases, the answer is the American side.
> VISUAL: E22_scale drops in; H09_guy_curly_neutral walks in from the left; C28_crowd_small pops in; G13_map_usa_grey pops in
That's not just a guess. Economists studied this carefully, using the tariffs America put on in twenty eighteen and twenty nineteen.

## CHAPTER 4: What the Studies Found
[calm] In twenty eighteen, the U.S. put new tariffs on hundreds of billions of dollars of goods, many of them from China. China answered with tariffs of its own.
> VISUAL: G13_map_usa_grey drops in; G14_map_china slides in from the right; G19_tariff_wall pops in; G19_tariff_wall pops in; E25_calendar pops in "2018"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China%E2%80%93United_States_trade_war | highlight "trade war"
Economists from the Federal Reserve, Princeton and Columbia looked at the prices of those imports. Before and after.
> VISUAL: E16_magnifying_glass pops in; H28_paper_stack drops in; D27_calculator_shocked pops in; H22_book_open pops in
They found that foreign sellers mostly did not cut their prices. Nearly the full cost of the tariffs was passed on to American importers and buyers.
> VISUAL: E33_factory pops in; H17 price tag pops in "same price"; G13_map_usa_grey drops in; H18 sign pops in "~100% PAID IN USA"
> SCREEN: National Bureau of Economic Research | https://www.nber.org/papers/w25672 | highlight "The Impact of the 2018 Trade War on U.S. Prices and Welfare"
One famous example was washing machines. In twenty eighteen, America put tariffs on imported washers. Within months, washer prices rose about twelve percent.
> VISUAL: H19_box_closed drops in "WASHER"; G19_tariff_wall pops in; H17 price tag pops in "+12%"; E37_cart_prices_up pops in
And here's the surprise. Clothes dryers had no tariff. But their prices went up too, by about the same amount. Stores sell washers and dryers as a pair.
> VISUAL: H19_box_closed pops in "DRYER"; H17 price tag pops in "+12%"; A07_narrator_confused slides in from the left; E49_exclamation_marks pop in

The tariffs did create some new factory jobs making washers in America. About eighteen hundred, by one estimate.
> VISUAL: E33_factory drops in; C28_crowd_small pops in "~1,800 JOBS"; A21_narrator_thumbs_up slides in from the left
But American buyers paid about one and a half billion dollars more a year for washers and dryers. That's roughly eight hundred thousand dollars for each job created.

## CHAPTER 5: Dan's Decision
[calm] Dan raises his price to two hundred fifteen dollars. He eats part of the tariff, and his customers pay the rest.
> VISUAL: H09_guy_curly_neutral walks in from the left; H17 price tag drops in "$215"; E22_scale pops in; C28_crowd_small pops in
Some customers grumble and buy anyway. Others walk out. His sales drop a little. His profit drops a lot.
> VISUAL: C28_crowd_small drops in; A12_narrator_angry pops in; A33_narrator_walking slides in from the right; D14_chart_arrow_down draws on
He decides not to hire the extra worker he'd planned for the holiday season. That's a cost of tariffs you'll never see on a receipt.
> VISUAL: H11_woman_shorthair_neutral walks in from the right; A48_narrator_stop_hand pops in; E25_calendar pops in "HOLIDAYS"; H18 sign pops in "NOT HIRED"
And here's another hidden effect. The American company that makes mixers in the U.S. sees that imported mixers now cost more. So it raises its own prices too.
> VISUAL: E33_factory drops in "MADE IN USA"; H17 price tag pops in; E45_arrow_up_big pops in; A05_narrator_suspicious slides in from the left
That American factory isn't paying any tariff. But it can charge more, because its competition got more expensive. Shoppers pay more, either way.
> VISUAL: E33_factory pops in; E02_money_bag pops in; C28_crowd_small drops in; E37_cart_prices_up pops in
And tariffs don't only hit finished goods. They also hit parts and raw materials. Steel, aluminum, chips, and plastic that American factories use to build their own products.
> VISUAL: H24_bricks_stack drops in "STEEL"; D23_ai_chip pops in; E33_factory pops in; G19_tariff_wall pops in

When a car maker pays more for steel, the price of the car can go up. So tariffs meant to protect one industry can raise costs for many others.

## CHAPTER 6: The Other Side of the Wall
[calm] Usually, they build a wall of their own. When America raised tariffs in twenty eighteen, China put tariffs on American goods, especially soybeans.
> VISUAL: G14_map_china drops in; G19_tariff_wall pops in; E39_grocery_bag pops in "SOYBEANS"; E31_cargo_ship slides in from the right
American farmers had sold huge amounts of soybeans to China. Almost overnight, much of that business dried up.
> VISUAL: C28_crowd_small drops in; E39_grocery_bag pops in; D14_chart_arrow_down draws on; E31_cargo_ship pops in
To help them, the government paid farmers billions of dollars in aid. More than twenty billion dollars in twenty eighteen and twenty nineteen.
> VISUAL: G09_capitol_building drops in; E02_money_bag pops in "$20 BILLION+"; C28_crowd_small pops in; A14_narrator_shrug slides in from the left
> SCREEN: Wikipedia | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_Facilitation_Program | highlight "Market Facilitation Program"
So a tax collected from American importers was partly used to pay American farmers hurt by the response to that tax. The money went in a circle.
> VISUAL: E02_money_bag pops in; red circle arrows; H09_guy_curly_neutral walks in from the left; C28_crowd_small pops in
This has happened before. In nineteen thirty, Congress passed the Smoot-Hawley Tariff, raising taxes on thousands of imports. Other countries fought back, and world trade shrank during the Great Depression.
> VISUAL: E25_calendar drops in "1930"; G09_capitol_building pops in; G19_tariff_wall pops in; E43_globe pops in; D14_chart_arrow_down draws on
> SCREEN: Wikipedia | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoot%E2%80%93Hawley_Tariff_Act | highlight "Smoot–Hawley Tariff Act"
Today, America's average tariff rate is the highest it has been in about a century, according to several economic estimates.
> VISUAL: D13_chart_arrow_up draws on; G19_tariff_wall pops in; E25_calendar pops in "~100 YEARS"; A45_narrator_head_shocked pops in
> SCREEN: The Budget Lab at Yale | https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research | highlight "Research"
In twenty twenty-six, the Supreme Court ruled that many of the tariffs imposed under an emergency law went beyond the president's power. Many importers were then due refunds.

So are tariffs always bad? Not exactly. And that's what makes them such a heated topic.

## CHAPTER 7: The Honest Answer
[calm] Supporters of tariffs have real arguments. They can protect industries that matter for national security, like steel or computer chips.
> VISUAL: G18_army_tank drops in; H24_bricks_stack pops in; D23_ai_chip pops in; A21_narrator_thumbs_up slides in from the left
They can push other countries to change unfair trade practices. And they can encourage some companies to build factories in America.
> VISUAL: G14_map_china pops in; E14_podium drops in; E33_factory pops in "NEW FACTORY"; G13_map_usa_grey pops in
Critics have real arguments too. Tariffs raise prices for shoppers and businesses. They can start trade wars. And they fall hardest on lower-income families, who spend more of their money on goods.
> VISUAL: E37_cart_prices_up drops in; G19_tariff_wall pops in; C10_worker_woman_stressed walks in from the left; E39_grocery_bag pops in
> SCREEN: Tax Foundation | https://taxfoundation.org/research/all/federal/trump-tariffs-trade-war/ | highlight "Tariff Tracker"
Both can be true at once. Tariffs can protect some jobs, while costing more money than those jobs are worth to the rest of the country.
> VISUAL: E22_scale drops in; C28_crowd_small pops in; E02_money_bag pops in; E33_factory pops in
Here's the takeaway. A tariff is a tax. It's collected from American companies. And most of its cost usually ends up with American businesses and shoppers, in higher prices, lower profits, or fewer jobs.
> VISUAL: A17_narrator_point_up slides in from the left; H18 sign drops in "A TARIFF IS A TAX"; H09_guy_curly_neutral walks in from the left; C28_crowd_small pops in
Whether that tax is worth it depends on what you're trying to protect. That's a real choice, and voters deserve to see the price tag clearly.
> VISUAL: E22_scale drops in; H17 price tag pops in; C28_crowd_small pops in; E16_magnifying_glass pops in
