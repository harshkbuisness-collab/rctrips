## CHAPTER 3: The worthless money bet

## CHAPTER 6: Genius, fool, or both?
[calm] A researcher named Stephen Gencarella argues that Dexter may have understood something real. Buying up goods nobody else wanted, and acting the fool on purpose.
> VISUAL: H05 narrator reading book; H22 book open pops in; E17 light bulb drops in; A06 narrator thinking
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Timothy Dexter | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timothy_Dexter | highlight "he understood the value of cornering the market on goods that others did not see as valuable"
Cornering the market means owning so much of something that you can control its price. If you own most of the whalebone in town when fashion demands it, you set the price.
> VISUAL: E10 chalkboard drops in; E27 crown pops in; E28 paper stack; H17 price tag drops in "YOU SET IT"; A38 narrator teacher pointer
And acting like a fool has a hidden benefit. People underestimate you. They do not see you as competition, and they tell you things they would never tell a serious rival.
> VISUAL: A03 narrator laugh; C02 banker greedy walks in; E51 speech bubble drops in; A37 narrator whisper
In a way, Dexter turned being laughed at into a business plan. Every joke against him was also free information about what other people expected to fail.

But there is another way to look at his story. It is called survivorship bias.
> VISUAL: E10 chalkboard drops in; H18 sign drops in "SURVIVORSHIP BIAS"; E16 magnifying glass pops in; A38 narrator teacher pointer
In World War Two, a statistician named Abraham Wald studied planes that came back from battle covered in bullet holes. The military wanted to add armor where the holes were.
> VISUAL: E42 airplane pops in; E25 calendar flips "1940s"; H16 sticky note drops in "HOLES"; A06 narrator thinking
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Survivorship bias | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survivorship_bias | highlight "the statistician Abraham Wald described methods of estimating the vulnerability of various parts of an aircraft"
Wald pointed out the problem. Those were the planes that survived. The planes hit in other places never came back to be counted.
> VISUAL: E42 airplane; E47 explosion drops in; red X on E42; E16 magnifying glass; A36 narrator mind blown
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Survivorship bias | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survivorship_bias | highlight "would overlook the question of where non-surviving planes were fatally hit"
Dexter is like a plane that came back. We remember him because his crazy bets worked. We do not remember the hundreds of other people who shipped useless cargo and simply went broke.
> VISUAL: C21 billionaire neutral; E31 cargo ship pops in; E20 trash can drops in; C28 crowd small; A06 narrator thinking
Survivorship bias can make us far too optimistic, because all the failures are quietly left out of the story.
> VISUAL: E28 trophy pops in; E20 trash can; red X on E20; E45 arrow up big; A35 narrator sweating
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Survivorship bias | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survivorship_bias | highlight "Survivorship bias may lead to overly optimistic beliefs because multiple failures are overlooked"
