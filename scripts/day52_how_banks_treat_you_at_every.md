## CHAPTER 3: Level one: forty dollars and the overdraft trap
[calm] At level one, the bank is happy to have Jasmine. But not for the reason you might think.
> VISUAL: D15 bank building pops in; C09 worker woman neutral; C02 banker greedy walks in; A05 narrator suspicious
One month, her rent comes out a day before payday. Her balance goes below zero. The bank pays the bill anyway, and charges her an overdraft fee.
> VISUAL: H23 apartment building pops in; E25 calendar flips; H17 price tag drops in "-$12"; H18 sign drops in "OVERDRAFT FEE"; A08 narrator shocked
Then a twelve dollar phone bill and a four dollar coffee go through that same day. Each one gets its own fee. By the weekend, she owes the bank more in fees than the bills themselves.

An overdraft is when you spend more than you have, and the bank covers the gap for a fee. One small coffee can end up costing thirty dollars or more.

In the US, banks can only charge overdraft fees on debit card and ATM purchases if you opt in. And according to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, people who opt in pay more than seven times as much in these fees.

## CHAPTER 4: Level two: ten thousand dollars and the credit card game
[calm] By her late twenties, Jasmine is a dental assistant, and she has saved ten thousand dollars. Suddenly, the bank's tone changes.
> VISUAL: E25 calendar flips "AGE 28"; C11 worker woman happy; H17 price tag drops in "$10,000"; D15 bank building pops in; A02 narrator happy
Monthly fees disappear, because her balance is above the minimum. And her mailbox fills up with credit card offers. Cash back, travel points, sign-up bonuses.

Where does the money for those rewards come from? Every time you swipe a card, the store pays a fee to the banks. It is called an interchange fee.
> VISUAL: D18 credit card pops in; F08 bg supermarket; E04 coin pile drops in; D15 bank building; A38 narrator teacher pointer
Fancy reward cards usually come with higher fees for the store. And stores often raise their prices a little for everyone to cover those fees.

These fees add up to huge numbers. Back in two thousand five, card fees paid by American merchants already totaled about thirty billion dollars a year.

## CHAPTER 7: Level five: twenty-five million and borrowing instead of selling
[calm] At the very top of the staircase, something strange happens. The richest customers often do not spend their own money at all. They borrow.
> VISUAL: E29 ladder; E27 crown pops in; C21 billionaire neutral walks in; E01 cash stack; A05 narrator suspicious
Here is why. Imagine someone owns stock that they bought for one million dollars, and it is now worth twenty-five million. If they sell it, they owe tax on the twenty-four million dollar gain.
> VISUAL: G11 stock exchange pops in; H17 price tag drops in "$1M"; H17 price tag drops in "$25M"; D26 tax form evil drops in; A38 narrator teacher pointer
So instead, they keep the stock and borrow against it. A loan is not income, so there is no tax on it. And for these customers, banks offer very low interest rates.
> VISUAL: E21 safe vault pops in; D15 bank building drops in; E01 cash stack; G04 percent happy; A37 narrator whisper
The loan is backed by the stock itself. If the stock keeps growing faster than the loan's interest, the family gets richer while living on borrowed money.
> VISUAL: G11 stock exchange pops in; E45 arrow up big; G04 percent happy; E02 money bag drops in; A36 narrator mind blown
Then there is one more piece. When someone dies, the assets they pass on usually get a new starting price for tax purposes, called a stepped-up basis.
> VISUAL: E23 hourglass pops in; H27 heart drops in; D25 retirement document; H18 sign drops in "STEP-UP"; A38 narrator teacher pointer
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Stepped-up basis | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stepped-up_basis | highlight "the asset receives a stepped-up basis , which is its market value at the time the benefactor dies"
That means the heirs can often sell without paying tax on all the gains that happened during their parent's lifetime.

## CHAPTER 8: What it means for you
[calm] So why does the bank treat Jasmine so differently at each level? Because to a bank, every customer is a mix of costs and profits.
> VISUAL: D15 bank building pops in; E22 scale drops in; E04 coin pile; E02 money bag; A38 narrator teacher pointer
Small balances cost money to serve, so banks make it up with fees. Large balances are valuable, so banks compete for them with perks and cheap loans.
> VISUAL: E04 coin pile pops in; H17 price tag drops in "FEES"; E02 money bag drops in; E27 crown; A15 narrator point right
You cannot jump to the top of the staircase overnight. But you can avoid the worst traps at the bottom.
> VISUAL: E29 ladder pops in; E44 warning sign drops in; A48 narrator stop hand; H27 heart
Turn off overdraft coverage if you do not need it, and set up low-balance alerts. Look for accounts with no monthly fees. Pay credit cards in full if you can, so rewards work for you instead of against you.
> VISUAL: E08 phone pops in; H18 sign drops in "ALERTS"; D18 credit card drops in; H16 sticky note drops in "PAY IN FULL"; A21 narrator thumbs up
And remember, every dollar you keep as a cushion moves you one step up the staircase. The first step is the hardest. After that, the bank starts working with you instead of against you.

Even a few hundred dollars in savings can be the difference between paying fees every month and paying none at all.
