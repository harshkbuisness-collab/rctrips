That number on the board is called an exchange rate. It is simply the price of one country's money, paid in another country's money.
> VISUAL: A38 walks in from the right with a pointer; E10 chalkboard slides in; the words "exchange rate = a price" are written on it letter by letter.

After World War Two, Japan's money was set at three hundred and sixty yen to one dollar. A yen was always meant to be a small coin, more like a penny than a dollar.

## CHAPTER 2: Money Is a Product
[calm] Every day, people around the world swap one currency for another. Most of them are not tourists like Sam.
> VISUAL: E43 globe spins in the center; small C28 crowds slide in from four sides; E51 speech bubbles with currency coins pop up over each group.
They are companies paying for parts, investors buying stocks and bonds, and banks moving money for their customers.
> VISUAL: E33 factory slides in from the left; E31 cargo ship sails in from the right; G11 stock exchange drops in from the top; arrows connect them one by one.
Like anything with a price, a currency gets more expensive when more people want it, and cheaper when more people want to get rid of it.
> VISUAL: A38 at E10; a simple supply and demand cross is drawn; G07 slides up the curve, then down; E45 and E46 flash in turn.
Here is an everyday example. When an American car dealer buys cars from Japan, the Japanese maker wants to be paid in yen in the end.
> VISUAL: E31 cargo ship docks; D22 car characters drive off the ship one by one; C09 worker woman at a dealership sign H18 waves.
So the dealer's dollars get swapped into yen somewhere along the way. That swap is a little bit of demand for yen.
> VISUAL: D01 walks to a small booth; it comes out the other side as G07; a small E45 arrow appears over G07.
Now flip it around. When a Japanese family buys an American phone or flies to New York, they sell yen and buy dollars.
> VISUAL: G15 map on the left, G13 map on the right; E08 phone and E42 airplane fly from right to left; G07 coins walk out of Japan and come back as D01.
Millions of swaps like these happen every second. Each one pushes the price a tiny bit up or a tiny bit down.
> VISUAL: E13 TV screen slides in with a ticking number; E24 clock spins fast; the number flickers up and down.
Then there are traders who never touch a car or a plane ticket. They buy a currency simply because they think its price will rise next week, and that moves the price too.
> VISUAL: H13 bowtie man slides in from the left with E07 laptop; a small chart on the laptop ticks up; he clicks and a G07 coin hops into his E06 briefcase.
> SCREEN: Federal Reserve, The International Role of the U.S. Dollar, 2025 Edition | https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/the-international-role-of-the-u-s-dollar-2025-edition-20250718.html | highlight the line that the dollar was on one side of about 88 percent of all foreign exchange trades in April 2022
And notice one thing on that page. The dollar is on one side of almost nine out of ten currency trades in the world.
> VISUAL: ten small D01 icons line up; nine light up green one after another; A08 shocked face pops in at the bottom right.
Trade matters. Tourists matter. But for a few years now, the biggest force pushing the yen down has not been cars or travelers at all.
> VISUAL: D22 and E42 slide off screen to the left; an empty stage remains; E52 thought bubble appears over H09.
It has been something much quieter. The price of borrowing money.

## CHAPTER 3: The Interest Rate Magnet
[calm] An interest rate is what money earns when you lend it out. If your bank pays you four percent, every hundred dollars grows to one hundred and four in a year.
> VISUAL: F03 bank lobby fades in; H09 hands E01 to C01 banker; G04 percent happy character jumps in from the top with "4".
Now imagine you are a big investor with money to park. Where would you put it? In the country that pays more, of course.
> VISUAL: C21 billionaire walks in from the right with E06 briefcase; three doors slide in, each with a G04 sign showing a different number.
> SCREEN: Federal Reserve press release, 16 September 2026 | https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20260916a.htm | highlight "raise the target range for the federal funds rate by 1/4 percentage point to 3-3/4 to 4 percent"
As of October 2026, America's central bank, the Federal Reserve, has set its key rate between three and three quarters and four percent.
> VISUAL: G10 central bank building with a US flag drops in on the left; G04 lands on top showing "3.75 to 4%".
> SCREEN: European Central Bank, Key ECB interest rates | https://www.ecb.europa.eu/stats/policy_and_exchange_rates/key_ecb_interest_rates/html/index.en.html | highlight the deposit facility rate of 2.50 percent from 16 September 2026
In Europe, the main rate is two and a half percent.
> VISUAL: a second G10 slides in at the center with G16 map behind it; a smaller G04 lands on top showing "2.5%".
> SCREEN: Bank of Japan, Change in the Guideline for Money Market Operations, 18 September 2026 | https://www.boj.or.jp/en/mopo/mpmdeci/mpr_2026/k260918a.pdf | highlight "around 1.25 percent"
And in Japan, even after a raise in September, it is about one and a quarter percent.
> VISUAL: a third G10 slides in on the right with G15 behind it; the smallest G04 lands on top showing "1.25%"; G05 frowns next to it.
So picture the money as water. It flows downhill from the place that pays the least to the place that pays the most.
> VISUAL: E43 globe tilts; small G07 coins slide down a ramp from the Japan side into a E21 vault on the US side; arrows show the flow.
For years, Japan paid almost nothing. Investors borrowed cheap yen, sold them, and bought dollars that earned more. Traders call this the carry trade.
> VISUAL: C21 walks to the Japan vault, picks up a G07 sack, swaps it at a booth for D01, and carries it to the US vault; label "carry trade" is written on H16 sticky note.
Every time someone did that, they sold yen. And when a lot of people keep selling something, its price falls.
> VISUAL: the G07 pile shrinks step by step; E46 arrow down drops in; the FRED line on E13 bends upward.
> SCREEN: FRED, DEXJPUS | https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DEXJPUS | mark July 2026 at about 164 yen per dollar, the weakest point for the yen in the chart since 1990
By the summer of 2026, one dollar bought almost one hundred and sixty-four yen. Sam's trip to Tokyo had become a bargain.
> VISUAL: H09 walks back in with A34-style celebration pose; E39 grocery bag and E40 coffee cup drop into his arms with small price tags.
But for Japan, a cheap yen is not all good news. Japan buys much of its oil, gas and food from abroad, and pays for most of it in dollars.
> VISUAL: E32 oil rig and D17 oil barrel slide in from the right; E39 grocery bag joins; each gets a H17 price tag that climbs upward.
So Japan's government has stepped in several times since 2022, selling dollars from its savings and buying yen to slow the fall.
> VISUAL: C05 politician at E14 podium; E21 vault opens; D01 bills fly out and G07 coins fly in; E44 warning sign blinks.
> SCREEN: Japan Ministry of Finance, Foreign Exchange Intervention Operations | https://www.mof.go.jp/english/policy/international_policy/reference/feio/index.html | highlight the list of monthly and quarterly releases, including 2024 and 2026

> VISUAL: E02 money bags stack up one by one to form a tower; a label "about 62 billion dollars" slides in; A08 watches.
It helps for a while. But you cannot fight interest rates forever with your savings. And Japan is not the only country that has tried to hold its price still.
> VISUAL: the money tower wobbles; A06 thinking face; a dotted line leads off the right edge of the screen.

## CHAPTER 4: The World's Favorite Money
[calm] In 1944, near the end of World War Two, leaders from forty-four countries met at a hotel in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire.
> VISUAL: E25 calendar flips to 1944; G13 map slides in; a small hotel E35 drops onto New Hampshire; C28 crowd walks in from both sides.
> SCREEN: Federal Reserve History, Creation of the Bretton Woods System | https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/bretton-woods-created | highlight the paragraph on fixed exchange rates tied to the dollar and gold
They agreed on a simple plan. Other currencies would be tied to the dollar, and the dollar would be tied to gold at thirty-five dollars an ounce.
> VISUAL: E05 gold bars drop in at the center; D01 attaches to them with a chain E55; G06-style coins and G07 attach to D01 with smaller chains, one by one.
This is when the yen got its three hundred and sixty. The dollar became the center of the whole system.
> VISUAL: "360" label from chapter 1 slides back in next to G07; E27 crown drops gently onto D01.
> SCREEN: Federal Reserve History, Nixon Ends Convertibility of U.S. Dollars to Gold | https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/gold-convertibility-ends | highlight "On the evening of August 15, 1971, Nixon addressed the nation"
Then, on August 15, 1971, President Nixon closed the gold window. Foreign governments could no longer swap their dollars for American gold.
> VISUAL: E13 TV screen slides in showing C05 at a podium; the E55 chain between D01 and E05 snaps; gold bars slide off to the left.
Within a couple of years, most big currencies began to float. That means their prices were set by buying and selling every day, the way we saw in chapter two.
> VISUAL: the smaller chains snap one by one; G06 and G07 float up like balloons and bob gently; E24 clock ticks.
> SCREEN: Federal Reserve History, The Smithsonian Agreement | https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/smithsonian-agreement | highlight the paragraph explaining that the new fixed rates broke down and currencies moved to floating
You might expect the dollar to lose its crown after that. But it found a new anchor. Oil.
> VISUAL: E27 crown wobbles on D01 and then holds; D17 oil barrel rolls in from the right and stops next to D01.
Oil is mostly bought and sold in dollars. So almost every country needs dollars to keep its lights on and its cars running.
> VISUAL: E32 oil rig rises from the bottom; E31 cargo ship tankers sail out; each country map G14, G15, G16, G17 slides in and holds a small D01.
In 1974, after the first oil shock, US Treasury Secretary William Simon flew to Saudi Arabia. Reporting by Bloomberg decades later described the deal that followed.
> VISUAL: E42 airplane flies across from left to right; E25 calendar shows 1974; C05 and a second figure shake hands in front of D17.
Saudi Arabia would put a large share of its oil dollars into US government bonds. Those dollars flowed back into America instead of leaving for good.
> VISUAL: D01 bills flow from America to an oil barrel, then turn around and drop into a D24 bond document on the US side; a circular arrow is drawn.
> SCREEN: IMF Data Brief on COFER, 30 September 2026 | https://data.imf.org/en/news/imf%20data%20brief%20september%2030 | highlight the US dollar share of 56.70 percent and the euro share of 20.60 percent for 2026 Q2
Today, central banks around the world keep their savings in many currencies. As of mid-2026, about fifty-seven percent of those savings are in dollars.
> VISUAL: a big pie chart drawn on E12 easel board; the dollar slice grows to just over half; D01 jumps into it happily.
About twenty-one percent are in euros. And only about five percent are in yen.
> VISUAL: G06 hops into a medium slice; G07 into a small slice; A06 nods at the board.
> SCREEN: European Central Bank, Introducing the euro | https://www.ecb.europa.eu/euro/intro/html/index.en.html | highlight "The euro was launched on 1 January 1999" and "legal tender in 21 of the 27 Member States"
The euro is young. It was launched in 1999, and today it is the money of twenty-one European Union countries.
> VISUAL: G16 map slides in; countries light up one by one with small G06 icons; E25 calendar flips to 1999.
So when Sam's dollar buys only eighty-nine euro cents, that is not a weak dollar. It is a strong euro, backed by a huge trading bloc and its own popular savings currency.
> VISUAL: H09 returns holding D01; G06 stands on a big base labeled with G16; E22 scale shows the two nearly balanced.
Because the dollar matters this much, some countries decided not to float at all. They tied themselves to the dollar on purpose.
> VISUAL: G06 and G07 keep floating on their balloons; a third coin D06 walks in from the right and clips a chain E55 to D01; A15 points at it.

## CHAPTER 5: Countries That Hold the Line
[calm] Sam's flight to Tokyo has a stopover in Hong Kong. At the airport shop, he notices something odd. The price of the Hong Kong dollar barely ever moves.
> VISUAL: E42 airplane lands; F07 city night background with E34 skyscrapers; H09 walks in from the left and stops at E15 desk; E13 shows a flat line.
> SCREEN: Hong Kong Monetary Authority, Linked Exchange Rate System | https://www.hkma.gov.hk/eng/key-functions/money/linked-exchange-rate-system/ | highlight "implemented in Hong Kong since 17 October 1983" and "HK$7.75-7.85"
Since October 1983, Hong Kong has kept its money between seven dollars seventy-five and seven dollars eighty-five for each US dollar. This is called a peg.
> VISUAL: E25 calendar flips to 1983; a small HK coin drawn as D06 is tied to D01 with E55 chain; two H18 signs drop in reading "7.75" and "7.85".
A peg works like a promise. The central bank says, if the price ever reaches the edge of the band, we will buy or sell as much as needed to push it back.
> VISUAL: C01 banker stands at each edge of a narrow road; whenever D06 drifts toward an edge, C01 pushes it back to the middle.
> SCREEN: HKMA, How Does the LERS Work? | https://www.hkma.gov.hk/eng/key-functions/money/linked-exchange-rate-system/how-does-the-lers-work/ | highlight the strong-side and weak-side Convertibility Undertakings at 7.75 and 7.85
On the October 9 Federal Reserve table, the Hong Kong dollar sat at about seven point eight five. Right at the edge, and still holding.
> VISUAL: the H.10 page reappears on E13; the Hong Kong row is circled; D06 sits on the "7.85" line while C01 holds it with one hand.
Saudi Arabia does the same thing. Its riyal has been held at three point seven five to the dollar since 1986.
> VISUAL: D17 oil barrel rolls in; a D06 coin with "3.75" tag is chained to D01; E25 calendar flips to 1986.
> SCREEN: Arab News, Confirmed: Riyal peg to dollar | https://www.arabnews.com/node/930961 | highlight the central bank's reaffirmed commitment to the 3.75 rate
For an oil seller, this makes sense. Its oil is sold in dollars, so a fixed price for its own money keeps life simple and predictable.
> VISUAL: E31 cargo ship tankers leave; D01 flows back in; a calm line on E13 stays flat; A21 thumbs up.
But a peg has a hidden cost. If your money is tied to the dollar, your interest rates have to follow America's, whether that suits you or not.
> VISUAL: G04 from the US G10 building walks across; a smaller G04 in Hong Kong copies its every move like a shadow; A05 suspicious face.
So when the Federal Reserve raised its rate in September 2026, banks in Hong Kong felt the pull right away, even though nobody there voted for it.
> VISUAL: E25 calendar flips to September 2026; G10 US building pulses; a thin rope from it tugs the small Hong Kong G04 upward; H12 shocked woman watches.
If America raises rates while your own economy is weak, you raise rates too. You have handed the steering wheel to someone else.
> VISUAL: D22 car character drives in; the steering wheel is held by a hand coming from the US side; H09 in the passenger seat looks worried.
And if investors ever stop believing the promise, the central bank has to spend its savings fast to defend it.
> VISUAL: E21 vault opens; D01 bills fly out; E44 warning sign drops in; the vault pile shrinks.
So every country makes a choice. Let the price float, like Japan and Europe, or lock it, like Hong Kong. Either way, someone wins and someone pays.
> VISUAL: E22 scale drops in; on one side G07 and G06 floating on balloons, on the other side D06 with a chain; the scale tilts slowly back and forth.
And that brings us to the question Sam never thought to ask. When the dollar is strong, who exactly is winning?
> VISUAL: H09 walks back in from the left; E48 question marks pop above him; D01 flexes on a E28 trophy base; G03 bull vs bear slides in small at the corner.

## CHAPTER 6: Strong or Weak, Who Wins?
[calm] For Sam, a strong dollar is wonderful. His hotel in Tokyo, his train tickets and his ramen all cost less in dollars than they did a few years ago.
> VISUAL: F04 city street becomes a Tokyo-style street; H09 walks in with E40 coffee cup; price tags H17 drop in and shrink one by one; A02 happy.
Americans who buy imported things win too. A strong dollar can make some foreign cars, electronics and clothes a little cheaper.
> VISUAL: E36 cart full rolls in; D22, E07 laptop and E08 phone hop into it; small E46 arrows appear on their price tags.
But now meet a different American. She works at a factory in Ohio that sells machines to Japan.
> VISUAL: C09 worker woman walks in from the right; E33 factory rises behind her; G13 map with Ohio highlighted slides in at the corner.
When the dollar is strong, her machines cost more in yen. Japanese buyers start looking at cheaper machines from somewhere else.
> VISUAL: a machine box H19 gets a price tag H17 in yen that grows taller; C13 retiree-like Japanese buyer turns away; C10 stressed face.
Fewer sales can mean fewer shifts and smaller raises. That is the hidden side of a strong dollar.
> VISUAL: E25 calendar with fewer marked shifts; D21 factory sad slides in over E33; E50 sweat drops on C10.
In Japan, it works the other way around. Japanese families pay more for imported fuel and food, because those are priced in dollars.
> VISUAL: G15 map slides in; E38 almost empty cart rolls in; D17 oil barrel with a rising tag; C14 retiree worried.
> SCREEN: Bank of Japan, Statement on Monetary Policy, 18 September 2026 | https://www.boj.or.jp/en/mopo/mpmdeci/mpr_2026/k260918a.pdf | highlight the lines mentioning exchange rates and the situation in the Middle East among the risks
But Japan's big exporters, the companies that sell cars and machines abroad, often earn more yen for every dollar they bring home.
> VISUAL: E31 cargo ship with D22 cars sails out of Japan; it returns with E02 money bags that turn into G07 piles; C11 happy worker.
So there is no simple good or bad. A strong currency helps the people who buy from abroad. A weak one helps the people who sell abroad.

It is a bit like the weather. A sunny day is great for the beach and bad for the farmer who needs rain. Same sky, different winners.

> VISUAL: E13 shows the broad dollar line rising and then easing; D01 climbs a E29 ladder and steps down a few rungs; A15 points.
> SCREEN: FRED, U.S. Dollars to Euro Spot Exchange Rate (DEXUSEU) | https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DEXUSEU | highlight October 2000 at about 0.83 and April 2008 at about 1.60

So the price on Sam's board is never final. It is a snapshot of millions of decisions, made by people chasing trade, safety and the best interest rate.

Which brings us back to the airport, and to the one hundred dollars still in Sam's hand.

## CHAPTER 7: Back at the Counter
[calm] Sam finally changes his money. Fifteen thousand eight hundred yen for Tokyo, and the rest kept for Paris.
> VISUAL: F04 airport street again; H09 at E15 counter; E01 slides across; G07 coins slide back; H09 tucks them in his pocket.
He now knows that the big yen number is mostly history. Japan chose a small unit long ago, and it never mattered as much as people think.
> VISUAL: "360" label and the E25 1949 calendar float by in a bubble E52 above H09.
He knows the real driver is demand. Who wants the money, for trade, for travel and, most of all, for the interest it can earn.
> VISUAL: three icons slide in one by one: E31 cargo ship, E42 airplane, G04 percent happy; each gets a check mark.
He knows the dollar sits at the center because the world prices oil in it, saves in it and trades in it, even after it left gold behind.
> VISUAL: D01 with E27 crown in the middle; D17, E21 vault and E43 globe orbit around it slowly.

And he knows that this center is slowly shifting. The dollar's share of world savings is still the biggest by far, but it has been drifting lower for years.
> VISUAL: the pie chart from chapter 4 returns on E12; the dollar slice shrinks by a hair; G06 slice grows a hair; A06 thinking.
None of this means you should trade currencies. It means the price of a vacation, a car or a tank of gas is linked to decisions made far away.
