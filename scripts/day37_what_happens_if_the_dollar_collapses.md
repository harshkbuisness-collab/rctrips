## CHAPTER 2: What a collapse really means
[calm] A currency collapse does not mean a dollar becomes worth zero. It means it loses a big chunk of its value, fast, compared to other money and to goods.
> VISUAL: D01 dollar happy; D03 dollar melting pops in; E46 arrow down big; E24 clock drops in; A38 narrator teacher pointer
Currencies move against each other every day. The dollar floats, which means its price is set by markets, not by a fixed rule.
> VISUAL: E22 scale pops in; G06 euro coin drops in; G07 yen coin drops in; D01 dollar happy; A15 narrator point right
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, United States dollar | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_dollar | highlight "has since floated freely on the foreign exchange markets"
Big swings have happened before. From nineteen eighty to nineteen eighty-five, the dollar rose about fifty percent against the other big currencies.
> VISUAL: E25 calendar flips "1980"; E25 calendar flips "1985"; D01 dollar happy; E45 arrow up big; H17 price tag drops in "+50%"; A36 narrator mind blown
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Plaza Accord | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plaza_Accord | highlight "the dollar had appreciated by about 50% against the Japanese yen"
In March nineteen eighty-five, the dollar hit its highest value ever against the British pound. That record held for more than thirty years.
> VISUAL: G16 map europe pops in; D01 dollar happy; E28 trophy drops in; E25 calendar flips "MAR 1985"; A36 narrator mind blown
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Plaza Accord | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plaza_Accord | highlight "the dollar reached its highest valuation ever against the British pound"
American factories were struggling to sell abroad, and Congress started talking about trade barriers.

Over the next two years, the dollar fell a lot. But it was planned, slow, and controlled. Americans barely noticed.
> VISUAL: E23 hourglass pops in; D01 dollar happy; E46 arrow down big; H27 heart; A02 narrator happy
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Plaza Accord | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plaza_Accord | highlight "depreciated significantly from the time of the agreement"

## CHAPTER 7: Why it is so unlikely for America
[calm] That is the biggest reason a sudden dollar collapse is so unlikely. When the world gets scared, it usually runs toward the dollar, not away from it.
> VISUAL: E43 globe pops in; C28 crowd small walks in; D01 dollar happy; E21 safe vault drops in; A17 narrator point up
Second, America borrows in its own currency. Germany, Zimbabwe and Argentina had debts or problems they could not solve by themselves. The US can always pay dollar debts in dollars.
> VISUAL: G09 capitol building pops in; D24 bond document; D01 dollar happy; E22 scale drops in; A38 narrator teacher pointer
Third, a top currency has a kind of gravity. The more people use it, the more useful it becomes, which makes it very hard for any rival to replace it.
> VISUAL: E43 globe; D01 dollar happy pops in; E27 crown drops in; G06 euro coin; G08 yuan coin; A06 narrator thinking
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Exorbitant privilege | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exorbitant_privilege | highlight "there are positive feedback effects associated with the exorbitant privilege that makes it hard for other currencies to become strong alternatives"
History shows that top currencies do lose their crown. But slowly. The Dutch had this privilege in the sixteen hundreds. Britain had it for much of the eighteen hundreds and early nineteen hundreds.
> VISUAL: E25 calendar flips "1600s"; G16 map europe pops in; E27 crown drops in; E25 calendar flips "1800s"; E23 hourglass; A38 narrator teacher pointer
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Exorbitant privilege | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exorbitant_privilege | highlight "The United Kingdom had exorbitant privilege for much of the 19th and early 20th century."
Each lost it over decades, not overnight. A slow slide is far more realistic than the dramatic Day One we imagined.
> VISUAL: E23 hourglass pops in; E46 arrow down big; E54 snail walks in; A14 narrator shrug

The real risks for the dollar are quieter. Big debts, high deficits, political fights over the Fed, and other countries slowly holding fewer dollars. Those would push the dollar down gently, over years.

## CHAPTER 8: What it means for the Parkers
[calm] So, back to the Parkers. In real life, their dollars are not about to vanish. But the thought experiment taught them a few useful things.
> VISUAL: C09 worker woman neutral; H09 guy curly neutral; C27 kid curious; H27 heart pops in; A18 narrator present right
First, a weaker dollar shows up as higher prices for imports, gas and travel. A stronger dollar does the opposite.
> VISUAL: D01 dollar happy; E37 cart prices up pops in; E42 airplane drops in; D17 oil barrel; A38 narrator teacher pointer
