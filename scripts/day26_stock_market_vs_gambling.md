## CHAPTER 1: What a share is
Since nineteen fifty-seven, the S and P five hundred has returned about ten percent a year on average, yet many people still call it gambling. Picture two people buying the same stock on the same afternoon. One wants to own part of a business for the next twenty years. The other plans to sell within ten minutes because the price chart looks promising.

A public company makes shares available for trading on stock exchanges under applicable rules. Buying a share doesn't mean you can walk into headquarters and take a chair home. It means you own a financial interest in the business.

Once shares are trading, most ordinary stock transactions happen between investors. If you purchase one share from another investor, the company typically doesn't receive that purchase price directly. You're exchanging ownership with someone who wants to sell.

## CHAPTER 2: Who's trading (types of players)
Buyers and sellers have different opinions about future profits, risks, interest rates, and alternatives. If many investors want a stock and few are willing to sell at the current price, bids may rise. If investors become pessimistic, prices can fall.

The price is not a machine calculating the company's one true value. It's the result of trading decisions influenced by information and expectations. Sometimes expectations are reasonable.

## CHAPTER 4: Long-term returns vs timing
Risk management becomes more important when investors use borrowed money. Imagine purchasing stocks with a margin loan. If prices rise, leverage can amplify the gains on your own capital.
> VISUAL: A01_narrator_neutral slides left; D13_chart_arrow_up drops; D14_chart_arrow_down draws; C01_banker_neutral pops right; G11_stock_exchange walks in; zoom; red circle; yellow highlight
If they fall, losses grow faster, and the broker may require additional funds or liquidate positions under the agreement. A broad market decline can become financially devastating for someone who borrowed heavily to buy assets. The same decline may be unpleasant but manageable for someone invested with money they won't need for years.

Economic life includes wages, employment, small businesses, and public services that aren't fully represented by the price of the largest stocks.

If one company collapses, the impact may be limited by diversification. But the entire market can still decline. Index funds also differ in cost, concentration, and construction.
