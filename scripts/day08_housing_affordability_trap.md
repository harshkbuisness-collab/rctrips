# Day 08: What's Really Keeping American Housing Prices High?
TITLES: What's Really Keeping American Housing Prices High? | Prices Were Supposed to Drop. They Didn't. | Why Nobody Can Buy a House in America

THREAD: Rates went up, so why didn't house prices come down?
STYLE: Casual Finance pattern (owner, 25 Oct 2026): what everybody knows -> the twist -> one key number -> the question.
Every line is a fact with its source said out loud, or a one-line turn. Pictures are planned per word in the beat sheet.

## CHAPTER 1: The sales that never happened
Everybody knows houses in America are expensive.
> SCREEN: FRED, Median Sales Price of New Houses | https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/MSPUS | highlight "Q2 2026: 410,700"
The typical new house sells for four hundred ten thousand seven hundred dollars.
And a thirty-year mortgage costs seven point three two percent.
> SCREEN: FRED, 30-Year Fixed Rate Mortgage Average | https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/MORTGAGE30US | highlight "2026-10-22: 7.32"
In January twenty twenty-one, that same loan cost two point six five percent.
So rates nearly tripled.
And when loans get that expensive, buyers disappear, and prices are supposed to fall.
Except they didn't.
Most people blame Wall Street buying up every house, or builders not building enough.
But while everyone is busy counting the buyers, nobody is counting the houses that never went up for sale.
That's the real problem.
In March twenty twenty-four, the Federal Housing Finance Agency counted them.
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "prevented 1.33 million sales between 2022Q2 and 2023Q4"
One point three three million American home sales that should have happened, and never did.
So why won't owners sell?

## CHAPTER 2: The golden handcuffs
Start with the loan almost every American owner has.
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "nearly all 50 million active mortgages have fixed rates"
The same paper says nearly all fifty million active mortgages in the United States have fixed rates.
The rate you sign is the rate you keep, for thirty years.
And in twenty twenty and twenty twenty-one, rates were the lowest in the history of that data.
Now say you owe three hundred thousand dollars at three percent.
You pay about one thousand two hundred sixty-five dollars a month.
Sell, and take the same loan at today's rate, and you pay about two thousand sixty.
That's almost eight hundred dollars more, every month, to live in a similar house.
So owners do the only thing that makes sense. Nothing.
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "the probability of sale is decreased by 18.1%"
The FHFA measured it: for every point today's rate sits above yours, your chance of selling drops by eighteen point one percent.
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "led to a 57% reduction in home sales with fixed-rate mortgages in 2023Q4"
By the end of twenty twenty-three, sales of homes with fixed-rate loans were down fifty-seven percent.
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "results in people not living in homes they would prefer"
The paper's own words: people end up not living in the homes they would prefer.
It's like a gym membership you never use, but you keep it because the old price will never come back.

## CHAPTER 3: Why prices went up
Here's where it flips.
When owners don't sell, there are fewer buyers, yes. But there are also far fewer houses.
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "which decreased prices by 3.3%"
The FHFA did the math on both sides. Higher rates on their own pushed prices down three point three percent.
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "The supply reduction increased home prices by 5.7%"
But the missing sellers pushed prices up five point seven percent.
So the tool built to cool prices froze the sellers instead. And prices went up.
But frozen sellers are only the last few years. America was short on homes long before that.

## CHAPTER 4: The homes nobody built
In two thousand five, American builders started about two million homes a year.
After the two thousand eight crash, that fell to four hundred seventy-eight thousand. The lowest since records began in nineteen fifty-nine.
It took until twenty twenty-one to get back above one and a half million.
> SCREEN: Freddie Mac Research | https://freddiemac.com/research/insight/housing-supply-still-undersupplied | highlight "the U.S. housing shortage has declined slightly to 3.7 million units"
Freddie Mac says the country is still short three point seven million homes.
> SCREEN: Freddie Mac Research | https://freddiemac.com/research/insight/housing-supply-still-undersupplied | highlight "Despite adding 5.8 million housing units over approximately four years"
And that's after adding five point eight million homes in about four years.
It's like running on a treadmill. Four years of sweat, same spot.
So why don't builders just build more?

## CHAPTER 5: The rules
In a lot of places, they're not allowed to.
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Single-family zoning | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-family_zoning | highlight "75% of land zoned for residential uses is zoned single-family"
In many American cities, seventy-five percent of the land for homes allows only one thing: a single house on its own lot.
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Single-family zoning | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-family_zoning | highlight "94% San Jose, California"
In San Jose, California, it's ninety-four percent.
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Single-family zoning | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-family_zoning | highlight "Single-family zoning both increases the cost of housing units and decreases the supply."
One house where four could fit means fewer homes, and each one costs more.
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, NIMBY | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NIMBY | highlight "opposition by residents to proposed real estate and infrastructure developments in their local area"
And when a builder asks to build more, neighbors often show up to stop it. It even has a name. Not in my back yard.
Most of those neighbors already own a home. And scarce homes stay valuable homes.
It's like the people already on the bus voting on whether the bus gets more seats.

## CHAPTER 6: So what about Wall Street?
Wall Street is real.
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Invitation Homes | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invitation_Homes | highlight "the company owned 86,192 rental homes"
Blackstone built Invitation Homes, which owned eighty-six thousand one hundred ninety-two rental homes by December twenty twenty-five.
But America has tens of millions of houses.
HousingWire checked the viral claim that investors bought forty-four percent of homes. Nowhere near it, they found.
The bigger competition is quieter.
> SCREEN: National Association of Realtors | https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40 | highlight "30% were all-cash buyers"
The National Association of Realtors says thirty percent of repeat buyers paid all cash.
> SCREEN: National Association of Realtors | https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40 | highlight "Gifts or loans from family and friends (22%)"
And twenty-two percent of first-time buyers used gifts or loans from family for the down payment. The bank of mom and dad.

## CHAPTER 7: Who gets locked out
> SCREEN: National Association of Realtors | https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40 | highlight "dropped to a record low of 21%"
First-time buyers made just twenty-one percent of home purchases. The lowest ever recorded.
> SCREEN: National Association of Realtors | https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40 | highlight "climbed to an all-time high of 40 years"
The typical first-time buyer is now forty years old. The oldest ever recorded.
> SCREEN: National Association of Realtors | https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40 | highlight "Delayed or denied homeownership until age 40 instead of 30 can mean losing roughly $150,000 in equity on a typical starter home."
NAR says buying at forty instead of thirty can cost about one hundred fifty thousand dollars of equity.
> SCREEN: National Association of Realtors | https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40 | highlight "Median time in home before selling: 11 years"
Meanwhile, the typical seller had stayed eleven years. Another record.
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "Certain borrower groups with lower wealth accumulation are less able to strategically time their sales, worsening inequality"
And the FHFA adds: people with less wealth are the least able to time a sale, which makes inequality worse.

## CHAPTER 8: What could unlock it
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Single-family zoning | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-family_zoning | highlight "In 2018, Minneapolis became the first major city in the US to end single-family zoning"
Some places changed the rules. In twenty eighteen, Minneapolis became the first major American city to end single-family zoning.
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Accessory dwelling unit | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accessory_dwelling_unit | highlight "SB 1069 and AB 2299 are California bills approved in 2016"
In twenty sixteen, California made it harder for cities to block small backyard homes.
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Assumable mortgage | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assumable_mortgage | highlight "allow the purchaser of a home to take over the existing terms of the mortgage including; the interest rate"
And some loans are assumable: a buyer takes over the seller's loan, low rate included. But most loans don't allow it.
Lower rates would free some owners, but they'd bring every buyer back at the same moment.
So the market didn't crash. It froze.
A forty-year-old first-time buyer, with help from mom and dad, buying from an owner who sat tight for eleven years.
We built a system that protects the people already inside it, and hands the bill to whoever is next in line.
