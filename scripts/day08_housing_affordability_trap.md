# Day 08: What's Really Keeping American Housing Prices High?
TITLES: What's Really Keeping American Housing Prices High? | Prices Were Supposed to Drop. They Didn't. | Why Nobody Can Buy a House in America
THUMBNAIL: US HOUSING — a USA map chained to a 3% ball while the price line keeps climbing
THREAD: Rates went up, so why didn't house prices come down?

## CHAPTER 1: The sales that never happened
[calm] In March twenty twenty-four, the Federal Housing Finance Agency published a strange number. One point three three million American home sales that should have happened, and never did.
> VISUAL: G13; D09; A08; "1.33 MILLION"
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "prevented 1.33 million sales between 2022Q2 and 2023Q4"
In those same years, mortgage rates more than doubled. So you'd think house prices fell. When loans get expensive, buyers disappear, and prices are supposed to come down.
> VISUAL: G05; D13; C28; D14; "PRICES SHOULD FALL"
Except they didn't. The typical new house in America still sells for over four hundred ten thousand dollars.
> VISUAL: D11; E45; A13; "$410,700"
> SCREEN: FRED, Median Sales Price of New Houses | https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/MSPUS | highlight "Q2 2026: 410,700"
And the thirty-year mortgage rate this week is still above seven percent.
> VISUAL: G05; D13; A45; "7.32%"
> SCREEN: FRED, 30-Year Fixed Rate Mortgage Average | https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/MORTGAGE30US | highlight "2026-10-22: 7.32"
Expensive loans. Expensive houses. At the same time. That's not supposed to happen.
> VISUAL: E22; D11; G05; A07; "BOTH HIGH?"
And the easy answers don't really work. It's not just Wall Street buying everything. It's not just that builders are slow.
> VISUAL: E34; H24; A05; E44; "NOT JUST..."
What's actually happening is much deeper. It's not about why buyers can't buy. It's about why sellers won't sell.
> VISUAL: A38; D24; D09; E55; "WHY WON'T THEY SELL?"
And if you rent, or you're saving for your first home, this is the reason your plan keeps getting pushed back.
> VISUAL: H23; E21; A17; E01; "YOU"
But for this to make sense, we first need to measure how far a house has moved away from your paycheck.
> VISUAL: A33; D11; E29; "HOW FAR?"

## CHAPTER 2: The price gap
[calm] In twenty twenty-five, the median American household earned about eighty-seven thousand dollars a year.
> VISUAL: C28; E01; A02; "$87,460"
> SCREEN: FRED, Median Household Income | https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/MEHOINUSA646N | highlight "2025: 87,460"
The median new house sold for about four hundred ten thousand. That's almost five years of your entire pay, before taxes, food, or anything else.
> VISUAL: D11; D27; E10; "4.7x INCOME"
> SCREEN: FRED, Median Sales Price of New Houses | https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/MSPUS | highlight "410,700"
In nineteen eighty-five, the same two numbers from the same government data gave about three and a half years. The house didn't just get pricier. It ran away from paychecks.
> VISUAL: E25; D13; A10; "3.5x in 1985"
But almost nobody pays cash. What decides if you can buy is the monthly payment. And that depends on the rate.
> VISUAL: A38; D09; G04; E01; "MONTHLY PAYMENT"
In January twenty twenty-one, the thirty-year rate fell to two point six five percent. The lowest in the history of that data.
> VISUAL: G04; D14; E25; A21; "2.65%"
Now do the math on a four hundred ten thousand dollar house with ten percent down. At two point six five percent, your payment is about one thousand four hundred ninety dollars a month.
> VISUAL: D11; E10; D27; "2.65% = $1,489"
At seven point three two percent, it's about two thousand five hundred forty. Same house. Same down payment. One thousand fifty dollars more, every single month.
> VISUAL: D27; A45; E50; "+$1,050 a month"
That's over twelve thousand dollars a year, for the exact same walls and the exact same roof. It's like your landlord raising the rent, except you're the one who owns the place.
> VISUAL: E01; D09; A22; "$12,600 a year"
So fewer buyers can pay that. Sellers should cut prices. But here's where the normal rule starts to break.
> VISUAL: A06; C28; D14; E48; "WHO OWNS?"

## CHAPTER 3: The first domino: golden handcuffs
[calm] The first domino is the loan most Americans already have. The normal home loan here is a thirty-year fixed rate. Your rate never changes, for thirty years.
> VISUAL: D24; E23; G04; A38; "30-YEAR FIXED"
The FHFA paper says nearly all fifty million active mortgages in the United States have fixed rates.
> VISUAL: G13; D24; C28; "50 MILLION"
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "nearly all 50 million active mortgages have fixed rates"
And in twenty twenty and twenty twenty-one, millions of owners bought or refinanced at around three percent. That cheap rate stays with them until they sell.
> VISUAL: G04; E21; D09; E55; "~3% LOCKED"
Now imagine you're one of them. You have a three hundred thousand dollar loan at three percent. You pay about one thousand two hundred sixty-five dollars a month.
> VISUAL: D27; E10; G04; "$1,265"
If you sell and take the same loan at today's rate, you pay about two thousand sixty. Almost eight hundred dollars more, every month, for a similar house.
> VISUAL: D27; G05; A45; "$2,061"
So you do the only thing that makes financial sense. Nothing. You stay.
> VISUAL: A48; D09; E55; "STAY"
The paper puts it plainly. Most of these loans sit far below today's rates, which creates a disincentive to sell.
> VISUAL: E55; D09; A48; "DON'T SELL"
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "most have interest rates far below prevailing market rates, creating a disincentive to sell"
And they measured it. For every one point that today's rate sits above yours, your chance of selling falls by eighteen point one percent.
> VISUAL: D27; D14; G05; "-18.1% per point"
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "the probability of sale is decreased by 18.1%"
By the end of twenty twenty-three, that lock-in alone had cut sales of homes with fixed-rate loans by fifty-seven percent.
> VISUAL: D14; G22; E46; "-57% SALES"
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "led to a 57% reduction in home sales with fixed-rate mortgages in 2023Q4"
People call these golden handcuffs. The rate is so good it keeps you in a house you might not even want anymore. Like keeping a gym membership you never use, because the old price will never come back.
> VISUAL: E55; E05; A14; "GOLDEN HANDCUFFS"
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "results in people not living in homes they would prefer"
And this is where it flips. When owners don't sell, there are fewer buyers, yes. But there are also far fewer houses for sale.
> VISUAL: C28; D09; E22; E46; "FEWER SELLERS"
The FHFA did the math on both sides. Higher rates on their own pushed prices down by three point three percent.
> VISUAL: D14; G05; A21; "-3.3%"
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "which decreased prices by 3.3%"
But the missing sellers pushed prices up by five point seven percent. The tool built to cool prices froze the sellers instead. And prices went up.
> VISUAL: D13; E45; A45; "+5.7%"
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "The supply reduction increased home prices by 5.7%"
But lock-in only explains the last few years. The country was short on homes long before that.
> VISUAL: D24; E16; A06; "SHORT ON HOMES"

## CHAPTER 4: The second domino: the homes nobody built
[calm] The second domino fell almost twenty years ago. In two thousand five, American builders started about two million homes a year.
> VISUAL: H24; D13; E33; "2,073,000 a year"
Then the two thousand eight crash hit. By April two thousand nine, new home starts had fallen to about four hundred seventy-eight thousand a year. The lowest since records began in nineteen fifty-nine.
> VISUAL: D12; D21; D14; "478,000"
And building didn't get back to one and a half million a year until twenty twenty-one. That's twelve years of under-building.
> VISUAL: E23; H24; E25; "12 YEARS"
Freddie Mac, the government-backed mortgage company, estimates the country is short three point seven million homes.
> VISUAL: G13; H24; A08; "3.7 MILLION SHORT"
> SCREEN: Freddie Mac Research | https://freddiemac.com/research/insight/housing-supply-still-undersupplied | highlight "the U.S. housing shortage has declined slightly to 3.7 million units"
America did build again. Five point eight million homes in about four years. But demand grew almost as fast, so the hole barely shrank.
> VISUAL: H24; E22; D09; A14; "+5.8 MILLION"
> SCREEN: Freddie Mac Research | https://freddiemac.com/research/insight/housing-supply-still-undersupplied | highlight "Despite adding 5.8 million housing units over approximately four years"
It's like running on a treadmill for four years. You're tired, you're sweaty, and you're standing in the exact same spot.
> VISUAL: A10; E23; H24; "TREADMILL"
So why don't builders just build more?
> VISUAL: E48; H24; A05; "WHY NOT BUILD?"

## CHAPTER 5: The third domino: the rules
[calm] Because in a lot of places, they're not allowed to. And this is where zoning enters the story. Zoning rules decide what can be built on each piece of land.
> VISUAL: G09; H22; E16; A05; "ZONING"
In many American cities, about seventy-five percent of the land for homes allows only one thing. A single detached house on its own lot.
> VISUAL: G13; D09; H23; A48; "75%"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Single-family zoning | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-family_zoning | highlight "75% of land zoned for residential uses is zoned single-family"
In San Jose, California, it's ninety-four percent.
> VISUAL: G13; D09; A45; "94% SAN JOSE"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Single-family zoning | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-family_zoning | highlight "94% San Jose, California"
The rule goes back to nineteen sixteen, in the Elmwood neighborhood of Berkeley, California.
> VISUAL: E25; H22; G13; "1916"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Single-family zoning | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-family_zoning | highlight "originated in 1916 in the Elmwood neighborhood of Berkeley, California"
And the effect is simple. When you can only build one home where four could fit, you get fewer homes, and each one costs more.
> VISUAL: D09; H23; D27; "1 instead of 4"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Single-family zoning | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-family_zoning | highlight "Single-family zoning both increases the cost of housing units and decreases the supply."
And when a builder asks to build more, local residents often show up to stop it. There's even a name for that. NIMBY. Not in my back yard.
> VISUAL: C28; E14; E51; A12; "NOT IN MY BACK YARD"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, NIMBY | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NIMBY | highlight "opposition by residents to proposed real estate and infrastructure developments in their local area"
Here's the quiet part. Most people at those meetings already own a home. And when homes stay scarce, their homes stay valuable.
> VISUAL: D09; D13; E22; A14; "SCARCE = VALUABLE"
It's like letting the people already on the bus vote on whether the bus gets more seats.
> VISUAL: E41; C28; A04; "NO MORE SEATS"
Now, about that other answer everybody reaches for. Wall Street.
> VISUAL: E34; E06; A05; "WALL STREET?"

## CHAPTER 6: Wall Street, and who still gets in
[calm] After the two thousand eight crash, millions of homes were foreclosed. Between twenty ten and twenty eleven, a company called Treehouse bought one thousand distressed houses in Phoenix, Arizona.
> VISUAL: G13; D12; E02; "1,000 HOUSES"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Invitation Homes | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invitation_Homes | highlight "bought 1,000 distressed houses in Phoenix, Arizona"
In twenty twelve, Blackstone bought it and formed Invitation Homes.
> VISUAL: E34; C04; E06; "BLACKSTONE 2012"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Invitation Homes | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invitation_Homes | highlight "The company was acquired by Blackstone Inc in the spring of 2012"
By December twenty twenty-five, it owned eighty-six thousand one hundred ninety-two rental homes.
> VISUAL: D09; G22; E28; "86,192 HOMES"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Invitation Homes | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invitation_Homes | highlight "the company owned 86,192 rental homes"
That sounds huge. But America has tens of millions of houses. Big investors matter a lot in some cities, like Atlanta and Phoenix, and much less in most others.
> VISUAL: G13; E22; A14; "SOME CITIES"
The bigger competition is quieter. It's people who already own a home and are sitting on years of equity.
> VISUAL: C21; E02; D09; "EQUITY"
The National Association of Realtors found that thirty percent of repeat buyers paid all cash.
> VISUAL: E02; E01; C04; "30% ALL CASH"
> SCREEN: National Association of Realtors | https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40 | highlight "30% were all-cash buyers"
And first-time buyers who did make it? Twenty-two percent used gifts or loans from family and friends for the down payment. The bank of mom and dad.
> VISUAL: D15; H27; E02; A14; "BANK OF MOM & DAD"
> SCREEN: National Association of Realtors | https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40 | highlight "Gifts or loans from family and friends (22%)"
So who is getting locked out?
> VISUAL: E55; C28; E48; "WHO?"

## CHAPTER 7: The people locked out
[calm] The same report gave two records on the same day. First-time buyers made up just twenty-one percent of all home purchases. The lowest share ever recorded.
> VISUAL: C28; D14; A41; "21%"
> SCREEN: National Association of Realtors | https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40 | highlight "dropped to a record low of 21%"
And the typical first-time buyer was forty years old. The oldest ever recorded.
> VISUAL: E24; H18; A45; "AGE 40"
> SCREEN: National Association of Realtors | https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40 | highlight "climbed to an all-time high of 40 years"
That share has shrunk by half since two thousand seven.
> VISUAL: D14; E46; E25; "-50% since 2007"
> SCREEN: National Association of Realtors | https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40 | highlight "contracted by 50% since 2007"
And waiting costs you real money. NAR's estimate: buying at forty instead of thirty can mean losing about one hundred fifty thousand dollars of equity on a starter home.
> VISUAL: E23; E01; D09; A35; "-$150,000"
> SCREEN: National Association of Realtors | https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40 | highlight "Delayed or denied homeownership until age 40 instead of 30 can mean losing roughly $150,000 in equity on a typical starter home."
Meanwhile, owners stay put longer than ever. The typical seller had lived in their home for eleven years. Another all-time high.
> VISUAL: D09; E55; E24; "11 YEARS"
> SCREEN: National Association of Realtors | https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40 | highlight "Median time in home before selling: 11 years"
And the FHFA paper adds one more line. People with less wealth are the least able to time their sales, which makes inequality worse.
> VISUAL: E22; C28; A41; "INEQUALITY"
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "Certain borrower groups with lower wealth accumulation are less able to strategically time their sales, worsening inequality"
So is anything actually working?
> VISUAL: E17; E48; A06; "ANYTHING WORKING?"

## CHAPTER 8: What could unlock it
[calm] Some places changed the rules. In twenty eighteen, Minneapolis became the first major American city to end single-family zoning.
> VISUAL: G13; H23; E20; A21; "MINNEAPOLIS 2018"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Single-family zoning | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-family_zoning | highlight "In 2018, Minneapolis became the first major city in the US to end single-family zoning"
California went after backyards. In twenty sixteen it passed laws that limit how much cities can block small backyard homes.
> VISUAL: E35; D09; G13; "BACKYARD HOMES"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Accessory dwelling unit | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accessory_dwelling_unit | highlight "SB 1069 and AB 2299 are California bills approved in 2016"
And some loans are assumable. A buyer can take over the seller's loan, including the old low rate.
> VISUAL: D24; E06; G04; "TAKE OVER THE RATE"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Assumable mortgage | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assumable_mortgage | highlight "allow the purchaser of a home to take over the existing terms of the mortgage including; the interest rate"
Those deals have grown since twenty twenty-two, for exactly this reason. But most loans don't allow it.
> VISUAL: D13; D24; A14; "MOST CAN'T"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Assumable mortgage | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assumable_mortgage | highlight "Mortgage assumptions have gained popularity since 2022"
And lower rates would free some owners. But they'd bring buyers back at the same moment. Rates alone can't fix a shortage.
> VISUAL: G04; C28; D13; E22; "BOTH COME BACK"
This is not financial advice. It's just how the machine works.
> VISUAL: A04; E22; D09
So here's the answer. Higher rates scared buyers away. But they froze sellers in place even harder. The market didn't crash. It froze.
> VISUAL: G05; C28; E21; A37; "IT FROZE"
So today, the typical first-time buyer is forty, often with help from mom and dad, buying from an owner who sat tight for eleven years.
> VISUAL: E24; D15; D09; E55; "40 / 11"
What we've built is a system that protects the people already inside it, and hands the bill to whoever is next in line.
> VISUAL: D09; E55; H15; A41; "THE BILL"
Thanks for watching Dollar Diaries.
> VISUAL: A23; D09; G13
