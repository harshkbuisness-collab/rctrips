# Day 08: What's Really Keeping American Housing Prices High?
TITLES: What's Really Keeping American Housing Prices High? | Prices Were Supposed to Drop. They Didn't. | Why Nobody Can Buy a House in America
THUMBNAIL: US HOUSING — a USA map chained to a 3% ball while the price line keeps climbing
THREAD: Rates went up, so why didn't house prices come down?

## CHAPTER 1: The sales that never happened
[calm] In March twenty twenty-four, researchers at the Federal Housing Finance Agency published a strange number. One point three three million home sales in America that should have happened, and never did.
> VISUAL: G13; D09; A08; "1.33 MILLION"
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "prevented 1.33 million sales between 2022Q2 and 2023Q4"
That's like every single home in Phoenix, Dallas and Denver deciding not to sell at the same time. Combined. And then some.
> VISUAL: G13; D09; E44; A04; "NOT FOR SALE"
At the same time, mortgage rates more than doubled. And the normal rule says that when loans get expensive, buyers disappear, and prices fall.
> VISUAL: G05; D13; C28; D14; "PRICES SHOULD FALL"
Except they didn't. The typical new house in America still costs over four hundred thousand dollars, which is very rude to everyone who waited for a crash.
> VISUAL: D11; E45; A13; "$410,700"
> SCREEN: FRED, Median Sales Price of New Houses | https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/MSPUS | highlight "Q2 2026: 410,700"
So everybody asks the same question. If nobody can afford a house, why are prices still so high?
> VISUAL: A06; E48; D11; C28; "WHY?"
And if you rent, or you're saving for a first home, or you have kids who will need one, this question is about you.
> VISUAL: H23; E21; A17; E01; "YOU"
Most people blame one villain. Wall Street. Or the Fed. Both are only half right.
> VISUAL: E34; G09; A05; E22; "HALF RIGHT"
The better question is a lot simpler. It's not why buyers can't buy. It's why sellers won't sell. And the answer is sitting in a drawer in almost every home with a mortgage.
> VISUAL: A38; D24; D09; E55; "WHY WON'T THEY SELL?"
But first, let's measure how far out of reach a house has moved.
> VISUAL: A33; D11; E29; "HOW FAR?"

## CHAPTER 2: The price gap
[calm] Start with income. In twenty twenty-five, the median American household earned about eighty-seven thousand dollars a year.
> VISUAL: C28; E01; A02; "$87,460"
> SCREEN: FRED, Median Household Income | https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/MEHOINUSA646N | highlight "2025: 87,460"
The median new house sold for about four hundred ten thousand. That's almost five years of a household's entire pay, before taxes, food or anything else.
> VISUAL: D11; D27; E10; "4.7x INCOME"
> SCREEN: FRED, Median Sales Price of New Houses | https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/MSPUS | highlight "410,700"
In nineteen eighty-five, the same two numbers from the same government data gave about three and a half years. So the house didn't just get pricier. It ran away from paychecks.
> VISUAL: E25; D13; A10; "3.5x in 1985"
But price is only half the story. Almost nobody pays cash. What really decides if you can buy is the monthly payment, and that depends on the mortgage rate.
> VISUAL: A38; D09; G04; E01; "MONTHLY PAYMENT"
In January twenty twenty-one, the average thirty-year mortgage rate fell to two point six five percent, the lowest in the history of that data.
> VISUAL: G04; D14; E25; A21; "2.65%"
By October twenty twenty-three it hit seven point seven nine percent. And this week, it's still above seven.
> VISUAL: G05; D13; A45; "7.32%"
> SCREEN: FRED, 30-Year Fixed Rate Mortgage Average | https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/MORTGAGE30US | highlight "2026-10-22: 7.32"
Let's do the math on that four hundred ten thousand dollar house with ten percent down. At two point six five percent, the payment is about one thousand four hundred ninety dollars a month.
> VISUAL: D11; E10; D27; "2.65% = $1,489"
At seven point three two percent, the same house costs about two thousand five hundred forty dollars a month. Same house. Same down payment. One thousand fifty dollars more, every month.
> VISUAL: D27; A45; E50; "+$1,050 a month"
That's over twelve thousand dollars a year, for the exact same walls and the exact same roof.
> VISUAL: E01; D09; A22; "$12,600 a year"
So the normal rule should kick in here. Fewer buyers can pay that, so sellers should cut prices. But to see why that didn't happen, we need to look at the people who already own.
> VISUAL: A06; C28; D14; E48; "WHO OWNS?"

## CHAPTER 3: The golden handcuffs
[calm] Here's the part most people miss. In America, the normal home loan is a thirty-year fixed rate. Your rate never changes, for thirty years.
> VISUAL: D24; E23; G04; A38; "30-YEAR FIXED"
The FHFA paper says it directly. Nearly all fifty million active mortgages in the United States have fixed rates.
> VISUAL: G13; D24; C28; "50 MILLION"
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "nearly all 50 million active mortgages have fixed rates"
And during twenty twenty and twenty twenty-one, millions of owners bought or refinanced at around three percent. Those cheap rates are now locked in, until they sell.
> VISUAL: G04; E21; D09; E55; "~3% LOCKED"
If they sell, the cheap loan disappears. Their next house comes with a new loan at today's rate. So even moving to a smaller, cheaper home can mean a bigger monthly payment.
> VISUAL: D09; E20; E35; G05; "SMALLER BUT PRICIER"
Here's what that looks like. A three hundred thousand dollar loan at three percent costs about one thousand two hundred sixty-five dollars a month. The same loan at seven point three two percent costs about two thousand sixty.
> VISUAL: D27; E10; G04; G05; "$1,265 vs $2,061"
So selling and buying again means paying almost eight hundred dollars more every month, just to live in a similar house. Most people look at that and say, no thanks, I'll stay.
> VISUAL: A48; D09; E55; "+$800 a month"
The paper's words: most of these loans have rates far below today's, which creates a disincentive to sell.
> VISUAL: E55; D09; A48; "DON'T SELL"
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "most have interest rates far below prevailing market rates, creating a disincentive to sell"
And they measured it. For every one percentage point that market rates sit above your own rate, your chance of selling falls by eighteen point one percent.
> VISUAL: D27; D14; G05; "-18.1% per point"
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "the probability of sale is decreased by 18.1%"
By the end of twenty twenty-three, sales of homes with fixed-rate loans were down fifty-seven percent because of this lock-in alone.
> VISUAL: D14; G22; E46; "-57% SALES"
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "led to a 57% reduction in home sales with fixed-rate mortgages in 2023Q4"
People call this golden handcuffs. Your cheap rate is so valuable that it keeps you in a house you might not even want anymore.
> VISUAL: E55; E05; A14; "GOLDEN HANDCUFFS"
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "results in people not living in homes they would prefer"
Now here's the twist. When owners don't sell, there are fewer buyers, yes. But there are also far fewer houses for sale. And the buyers who are left still fight over that short list.
> VISUAL: C28; D09; E22; E46; "FEWER SELLERS"
The FHFA did the math on both sides. Higher rates on their own pushed prices down by three point three percent.
> VISUAL: D14; G05; A21; "-3.3%"
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "which decreased prices by 3.3%"
But the missing supply pushed prices up by five point seven percent. The handcuffs won. So prices went up.
> VISUAL: D13; E45; A45; "+5.7%"
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "The supply reduction increased home prices by 5.7%"
That's the drawer we talked about. The mortgage paper inside it. But lock-in only explains the last few years. The country was short on homes long before that.
> VISUAL: D24; E16; A06; "SHORT ON HOMES"

## CHAPTER 4: The homes nobody built
[calm] In two thousand five, American builders started about two million homes a year.
> VISUAL: H24; D13; E33; "2,073,000 a year"
Then the two thousand eight crash hit. Builders went bust. By April two thousand nine, new home starts had fallen to about four hundred seventy-eight thousand a year, the lowest since records began in nineteen fifty-nine.
> VISUAL: D12; D21; D14; "478,000"
And it stayed low for a long time. Building didn't get back to one and a half million a year until twenty twenty-one. That's twelve years of under-building.
> VISUAL: E23; H24; E25; "12 YEARS"
Meanwhile, millions of millennials hit their thirties. That's exactly the age people usually buy their first home.
> VISUAL: C28; E25; D09; E45; "AGE 30+"
Freddie Mac, the government-backed mortgage company, estimates the country is short three point seven million homes.
> VISUAL: G13; H24; A08; "3.7 MILLION SHORT"
> SCREEN: Freddie Mac Research | https://freddiemac.com/research/insight/housing-supply-still-undersupplied | highlight "the U.S. housing shortage has declined slightly to 3.7 million units"
And here's the frustrating part. America did build again. Five point eight million homes in about four years. But demand grew by almost the same amount, so the hole barely shrank.
> VISUAL: H24; E22; D09; A14; "+5.8 MILLION"
> SCREEN: Freddie Mac Research | https://freddiemac.com/research/insight/housing-supply-still-undersupplied | highlight "Despite adding 5.8 million housing units over approximately four years"
It's like running on a treadmill. You can sweat for four years and still be standing in the same spot.
> VISUAL: A10; E23; H24; "TREADMILL"
Other groups give smaller or bigger numbers. But nearly everyone agrees on the direction. Too few homes. So why don't builders just build more?
> VISUAL: E48; H24; A05; "WHY NOT BUILD?"

## CHAPTER 5: The rules that block building
[calm] In many places, they're not allowed to. Every American city has zoning rules that decide what can be built on each piece of land.
> VISUAL: G09; H22; E16; A05; "ZONING"
And in many American cities, about seventy-five percent of the land for homes allows only one thing. A single detached house on its own lot. No duplex. No small apartment building.
> VISUAL: G13; D09; H23; A48; "75%"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Single-family zoning | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-family_zoning | highlight "75% of land zoned for residential uses is zoned single-family"
In San Jose, California, it's ninety-four percent. In Los Angeles, seventy-five. In Portland, Oregon, seventy-seven.
> VISUAL: G13; D09; A45; "94% SAN JOSE"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Single-family zoning | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-family_zoning | highlight "94% San Jose, California"
Where did that come from? Wikipedia traces it to nineteen sixteen, in the Elmwood neighborhood of Berkeley, California.
> VISUAL: E25; H22; G13; "1916"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Single-family zoning | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-family_zoning | highlight "originated in 1916 in the Elmwood neighborhood of Berkeley, California"
It was used there to keep minorities out of white neighborhoods. A century later, the rule stayed, and today it mostly keeps new homes out.
> VISUAL: H22; A48; D09; E44; "STILL HERE"
The effect is simple. When you can only build one home where four could fit, you get fewer homes, and each one costs more.
> VISUAL: D09; H23; D27; "1 instead of 4"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Single-family zoning | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-family_zoning | highlight "Single-family zoning both increases the cost of housing units and decreases the supply."
And when a builder does ask to build more, local residents often show up at city meetings to stop it. There's even a name for that. NIMBY. Not in my back yard.
> VISUAL: C28; E14; E51; A12; "NOT IN MY BACK YARD"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, NIMBY | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NIMBY | highlight "opposition by residents to proposed real estate and infrastructure developments in their local area"
Here's the uncomfortable part. Most of the people at those meetings already own a home. And when homes stay scarce, their homes stay valuable.
> VISUAL: D09; D13; E22; A14; "SCARCE = VALUABLE"
So the people inside the market vote on how many new people get in. Kind of like letting the people already on the bus decide if the bus gets more seats.
> VISUAL: E41; C28; A04; "NO MORE SEATS"
Now, about that other villain everybody talks about. Wall Street.
> VISUAL: E34; E06; A05; "WALL STREET?"

## CHAPTER 6: Wall Street and family money
[calm] After the two thousand eight crash, millions of homes were foreclosed. And big investors saw a bargain.
> VISUAL: D12; E02; C04; "FORECLOSED"
Between twenty ten and twenty eleven, a company called Treehouse bought one thousand distressed houses in Phoenix, Arizona.
> VISUAL: G13; D12; E02; "1,000 HOUSES"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Invitation Homes | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invitation_Homes | highlight "bought 1,000 distressed houses in Phoenix, Arizona"
In the spring of twenty twelve, Blackstone, one of the biggest investment firms in the world, bought it and formed Invitation Homes.
> VISUAL: E34; C04; E06; "BLACKSTONE 2012"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Invitation Homes | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invitation_Homes | highlight "The company was acquired by Blackstone Inc in the spring of 2012"
By December twenty twenty-five, Invitation Homes owned eighty-six thousand one hundred ninety-two rental houses. The largest owner of single-family rentals in the country.
> VISUAL: D09; G22; E28; "86,192 HOMES"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Invitation Homes | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invitation_Homes | highlight "the company owned 86,192 rental homes"
Its biggest market is Atlanta, which brought in almost thirteen percent of its revenue in twenty twenty-five.
> VISUAL: G13; E02; G22; "ATLANTA 12.9%"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Invitation Homes | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invitation_Homes | highlight "The company's largest markets are Atlanta (12.9% of 2025 revenue)"
That sounds huge. But America has tens of millions of houses. So big investors matter a lot in some cities, like Atlanta and Phoenix, and very little in most others. Half right.
> VISUAL: G13; E22; A14; "HALF RIGHT"
The bigger competition is quieter. It's buyers who already own a home and have built up a pile of equity.
> VISUAL: C21; E02; D09; "EQUITY"
The National Association of Realtors found that thirty percent of repeat buyers paid all cash in its twenty twenty-five report.
> VISUAL: E02; E01; C04; "30% ALL CASH"
> SCREEN: National Association of Realtors | https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40 | highlight "30% were all-cash buyers"
Their own economist called it a tale of two cities. Owners with equity make big down payments and cash offers, while first-time buyers struggle to get in.
> VISUAL: E22; D09; H23; A06; "TALE OF TWO CITIES"
> SCREEN: National Association of Realtors | https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40 | highlight "We're seeing buyers with significant housing equity making larger down payments and all-cash offers, while first-time buyers continue to struggle to enter the market."
And for first-time buyers who do make it, more than one in five used gifts or loans from family and friends for the down payment. The bank of mom and dad.
> VISUAL: D15; H27; E02; A14; "BANK OF MOM & DAD"
> SCREEN: National Association of Realtors | https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40 | highlight "Gifts or loans from family and friends (22%)"
So who is actually getting locked out?
> VISUAL: E55; C28; E48; "WHO?"

## CHAPTER 7: The people locked out
[calm] The same report, from November twenty twenty-five, gave two records on the same day.
> VISUAL: E25; E10; A08; "2 RECORDS"
First-time buyers made up just twenty-one percent of all home purchases. The lowest share ever recorded.
> VISUAL: C28; D14; A41; "21%"
> SCREEN: National Association of Realtors | https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40 | highlight "dropped to a record low of 21%"
And the typical first-time buyer was forty years old. The oldest ever recorded.
> VISUAL: E24; H18; A45; "AGE 40"
> SCREEN: National Association of Realtors | https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40 | highlight "climbed to an all-time high of 40 years"
NAR says the share of first-time buyers has shrunk by half since two thousand seven.
> VISUAL: D14; E46; E25; "-50% since 2007"
> SCREEN: National Association of Realtors | https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40 | highlight "contracted by 50% since 2007"
And waiting costs real money. Their estimate: buying at forty instead of thirty can mean losing about one hundred fifty thousand dollars of equity on a typical starter home.
> VISUAL: E23; E01; D09; A35; "-$150,000"
> SCREEN: National Association of Realtors | https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40 | highlight "Delayed or denied homeownership until age 40 instead of 30 can mean losing roughly $150,000 in equity on a typical starter home."
Meanwhile, owners are staying put longer than ever. The typical seller had lived in their home for eleven years, another all-time high.
> VISUAL: D09; E55; E24; "11 YEARS"
> SCREEN: National Association of Realtors | https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40 | highlight "Median time in home before selling: 11 years"
And the FHFA paper adds one more line. People with less wealth are the least able to time their sales well, which makes inequality worse.
> VISUAL: E22; C28; A41; "INEQUALITY"
> SCREEN: FHFA Working Paper 24-03 | https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403 | highlight "Certain borrower groups with lower wealth accumulation are less able to strategically time their sales, worsening inequality"
So is anything actually working?
> VISUAL: E17; E48; A06; "ANYTHING WORKING?"

## CHAPTER 8: What could unlock it
[calm] Some places changed the rules. In twenty eighteen, Minneapolis became the first major American city to end single-family zoning, and allowed duplexes and triplexes in every neighborhood.
> VISUAL: G13; H23; E20; A21; "MINNEAPOLIS 2018"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Single-family zoning | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-family_zoning | highlight "In 2018, Minneapolis became the first major city in the US to end single-family zoning"
California went after backyards. In twenty sixteen it passed laws that limit how much cities can block small backyard homes, called accessory dwelling units.
> VISUAL: E35; D09; G13; "BACKYARD HOMES"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Accessory dwelling unit | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accessory_dwelling_unit | highlight "SB 1069 and AB 2299 are California bills approved in 2016"
Then there's the loan itself. Some mortgages, like many government-backed ones, are assumable. A buyer can take over the seller's loan, including the old low rate.
> VISUAL: D24; E06; G04; "TAKE OVER THE RATE"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Assumable mortgage | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assumable_mortgage | highlight "allow the purchaser of a home to take over the existing terms of the mortgage including; the interest rate"
Those deals have gained popularity since twenty twenty-two, for exactly this reason. But most loans don't allow it.
> VISUAL: D13; D24; A14; "MOST CAN'T"
> SCREEN: Wikipedia, Assumable mortgage | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assumable_mortgage | highlight "Mortgage assumptions have gained popularity since 2022"
And lower rates would unlock some owners. But they'd also bring buyers back at the same moment. Rates alone can't fix a shortage.
> VISUAL: G04; C28; D13; E22; "BOTH COME BACK"
The slowest fix is time. Jobs change, families grow, people retire. Every year a few more handcuffs come off.
> VISUAL: E23; E55; E06; A06; "TIME"
This is not financial advice. It's just how the machine works.
> VISUAL: A04; E22; D09
So here's the answer. Prices didn't fall because the people who own homes have every reason to stay, and the rules make it slow to build new ones.
> VISUAL: E55; H24; G09; D11; A18
Higher rates scared buyers away. But they also froze sellers in place. The market didn't crash. It froze.
> VISUAL: G05; C28; E21; A37; "IT FROZE"
Remember those one point three three million sales that never happened? They're still out there. Locked in a drawer, waiting for the rate to change.
> VISUAL: D24; D09; E23; A06; "1.33 MILLION"
Thanks for watching Dollar Diaries.
> VISUAL: A23; D09; G13
