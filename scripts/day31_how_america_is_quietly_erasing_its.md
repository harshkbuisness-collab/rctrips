## CHAPTER 2: What the debt really is
[calm] First, what is the national debt? It is every dollar the federal government has borrowed and not yet paid back.

The Treasury itself explains it with a simple picture. It is like using a credit card and never paying off the full balance.

America has carried debt since the very beginning. The Revolutionary War left the new country owing seventy-five million dollars, much of it borrowed from France.

## CHAPTER 5: Tools two and three: inflation and low rates
[serious] The second tool is inflation. Inflation means prices go up, so each dollar buys less.
> VISUAL: E37 cart prices up pops in; D03 dollar melting drops in; H17 price tag drops in "PRICES UP"; A35 narrator sweating
Here is why the government likes it. Debt is fixed in dollars. If prices double, the government still owes the same number of dollars.
> VISUAL: D24 bond document pops in; E01 cash stack; E37 cart prices up; A04 narrator smug
Let's do the math with a simple loan. You owe one hundred dollars. Prices rise five percent a year. After ten years, that hundred dollars only buys what about sixty-one dollars bought when you borrowed it.
> VISUAL: D27 calculator shocked pops in; H17 price tag drops in "$100"; H17 price tag drops in "$61"; E25 calendar flips; A38 narrator teacher pointer
The borrower wins. The lender loses. And in this story, the borrower is the government. The lender is Walter.
> VISUAL: G09 capitol building pops in; C13 retiree neutral; E22 scale drops in; A12 narrator angry
But inflation alone does not do the trick. If prices rise, lenders usually demand higher interest to make up for it.
> VISUAL: G05 percent angry pops in; C01 banker neutral walks in; E45 arrow up big; A06 narrator thinking
That is where the third tool comes in. Keep interest rates low on purpose, even while prices are rising.
> VISUAL: C25 central banker printer pops in; G04 percent happy drops in; E46 arrow down big; A37 narrator whisper
During the war, the Federal Reserve promised to keep the rate on short-term government debt fixed at just three eighths of one percent.
> VISUAL: G10 central bank building slides in from the right; H17 price tag drops in "0.375%"; G18 army tank; A15 narrator point right

Economists have a name for this mix. They call it financial repression. Rates are held below inflation, so savers slowly lose value without noticing.

A real interest rate is the rate you earn minus inflation. If your bond pays two percent and prices rise four percent, you are really losing two percent a year.
> VISUAL: D27 calculator shocked pops in; H16 sticky note drops in "2% - 4%"; D02 dollar sad drops in; A35 narrator sweating

> VISUAL: D28 debt monster pops in; D03 dollar melting drops in; E46 arrow down big; A36 narrator mind blown
