## CHAPTER 2: Rule One: Income, Not Wealth
[calm] America's income tax is a tax on income. Money that comes to you during the year. Wages, interest, business profits, and gains when you sell something.
> VISUAL: A38_narrator_teacher_pointer slides in from the left; E01_cash_stack drops in; G04_percent_happy pops in; E33_factory pops in; H17 price tag pops in
> SCREEN: Internal Revenue Service | https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc409 | highlight "Capital gains and losses"
It doesn't tax wealth itself. What you own isn't taxed each year just because it got more valuable.
> VISUAL: E21_safe_vault drops in; E05_gold_bars pops in; D09_house_happy pops in; A48_narrator_stop_hand slides in from the left
This is something almost everyone benefits from. If your house goes up in value, you don't pay income tax on that. Not until you sell, and often not even then.
> VISUAL: D09_house_happy drops in; D13_chart_arrow_up draws on; D26_tax_form_evil pops in; A21_narrator_thumbs_up slides in from the left
And for most families, that's a good thing. Imagine getting a tax bill every year just because your home's value went up, even though you never got any cash.
> VISUAL: D09_house_happy drops in; D26_tax_form_evil pops in; E48_question_marks pop in; C10_worker_woman_stressed walks in from the left
The same is true for stocks. If your shares rise, that growth is called an unrealized gain. It only becomes taxable when you sell. That's called realizing the gain.

## CHAPTER 3: Rule Two: Borrow, Don't Sell
[calm] Here's rule two. Borrowed money isn't income. When you take out a loan, you don't pay tax on it, because you have to pay it back.
> VISUAL: A38_narrator_teacher_pointer slides in from the left; D15_bank_building drops in; E02_money_bag pops in; D26_tax_form_evil pops in "NOT INCOME"
That's true for everyone. Your car loan isn't taxed. Neither is your mortgage. And neither is Mark's loan.
> VISUAL: E41_car drops in; D09_house_happy pops in; C21_billionaire_neutral walks in from the right; A21_narrator_thumbs_up slides in from the left
So instead of selling his shares, Mark goes to a bank and borrows against them. He puts up his stock as a guarantee, like a giant pawn shop.
> VISUAL: C21_billionaire_neutral walks in from the right; D24_bond_document pops in; D15_bank_building drops in; E21_safe_vault pops in
Banks are happy to do it. Mark is super rich and his stock is valuable. So they give him a very low interest rate, often much lower than Sarah's car loan.

> SCREEN: FINRA | https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/securities-backed-lines-credit | highlight "Securities-Backed Lines of Credit"
For the bank, it's an easy, safe deal. If Mark ever stopped paying, the bank could simply sell some of his shares to get its money back.

## CHAPTER 4: Rule Three: The Reset at Death
[calm] Rule three has a strange name. The step-up in basis. It's one of the most important tax rules most people have never heard of.
> VISUAL: A38_narrator_teacher_pointer slides in from the left; E29_ladder drops in; H18 sign pops in "STEP-UP IN BASIS"; E48_question_marks pop in
> SCREEN: Internal Revenue Service | https://www.irs.gov/faqs/interest-dividends-other-types-of-income/gifts-inheritances | highlight "Gifts & Inheritances"
Your basis is basically what you paid for something. If you buy stock for one dollar and sell it for one hundred, your taxable gain is ninety-nine dollars.
> VISUAL: H17 price tag pops in "BOUGHT $1"; H17 price tag pops in "SOLD $100"; D27_calculator_shocked drops in; H16 sticky note pops in "GAIN $99"
But when someone dies, the basis of what they leave behind resets to its value on the day they die.
> VISUAL: E23_hourglass drops in; D24_bond_document pops in; E29_ladder pops in; H18 sign pops in "RESET"
Say Mark's shares cost him almost nothing when he started the company. When he dies, they're worth five hundred million dollars.
> VISUAL: C21_billionaire_neutral walks in from the right; H17 price tag pops in "$0"; H17 price tag pops in "$500 MILLION"; D13_chart_arrow_up draws on
His kids inherit the shares, and their basis resets to five hundred million. If they sell the next day, the taxable gain is zero. The whole lifetime of growth is never taxed as income.
> VISUAL: C27_kid_curious walks in from the left; D24_bond_document pops in; H18 sign pops in "GAIN = $0"; A45_narrator_head_shocked pops in
For Sarah, it works the same way with small amounts. If her grandma leaves her a few stocks, the basis resets too. The rule isn't only for the rich. But the rich get by far the biggest benefit.
> VISUAL: C09_worker_woman_neutral walks in from the left; C15_retiree_happy walks in from the right; D24_bond_document pops in; E22_scale drops in
They can sell some shares and use the money to pay off the loans Mark took out while he was alive.
> VISUAL: D24_bond_document pops in; E01_cash_stack drops in; D15_bank_building pops in; red arrow to the bank
Financial advisers have a nickname for this whole strategy. Buy, borrow, die. Buy assets that grow. Borrow against them. And hold them until death.
> VISUAL: H16 sticky note pops in "BUY"; H16 sticky note pops in "BORROW"; H16 sticky note pops in "DIE"; A04_narrator_smug slides in from the left
There is one tax that can still apply at death. The estate tax. But in twenty twenty-six, it only applies above fifteen million dollars per person, and wealthy families plan carefully to shrink what's counted.

> SCREEN: Internal Revenue Service | https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/estate-tax | highlight "Estate Tax"
So even for families who do pay estate tax, it often reaches only part of the fortune. And this isn't the only gap. There's one more that hits Sarah directly.

## CHAPTER 5: Why Sarah's Rate Is Higher
[calm] When Mark does sell stock he's held for over a year, the gain is taxed at a special lower rate. The top rate is twenty percent, plus a small extra tax.

The top rate on wages, like Sarah's if she earned much more, is thirty-seven percent. So a dollar earned by working can be taxed more than a dollar earned by investing.

## CHAPTER 6: The Other Side
