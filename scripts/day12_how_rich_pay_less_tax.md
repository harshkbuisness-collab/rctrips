## CHAPTER 2: Capital gains vs wages
Not all business owners pay less than employees, and tax rates cannot be reduced to one simple comparison. But the timing of when assets are sold makes a tremendous difference to when taxable income appears. Now suppose Emma wants money for a house or another investment without selling shares.

If Emma can borrow rather than sell, might she avoid realizing a taxable gain for a very long time? Under current U.S. rules, inherited assets often receive a tax basis tied to their fair market value at the owner's death, subject to exceptions and other rules. Tax basis is roughly the value used to calculate taxable gains when an asset is sold.
> VISUAL: A15_narrator_point_right slides left; D09_house_happy drops; D11_house_expensive draws; E35_small_house pops right; G21_landlord walks in; zoom; red circle; yellow highlight
If shares bought cheaply have risen enormously, and an heir receives a new basis, certain appreciation may escape individual capital-gains tax that would have applied to a sale during life. Estate taxes, state taxes, trusts, and other rules may still matter. The system is more complicated than a three-word slogan, but the timing effect is real.
> VISUAL: A18_narrator_present_right slides left; D13_chart_arrow_up drops; D14_chart_arrow_down draws; C01_banker_neutral pops right; G11_stock_exchange walks in; zoom; red circle; yellow highlight

This is a hypothetical example, not a claim about any particular billionaire. If she sells the shares, the gain generally becomes relevant for income-tax purposes, subject to basis calculations and other rules.

## CHAPTER 4: Step-up in basis at death
Founders may retain shares because they believe the company has more growth ahead, because they need voting control, or because selling is restricted. A policy that changes the timing or taxation of gains can influence these decisions. It might improve perceived fairness while changing incentives for entrepreneurship, risk-taking, or portfolio diversification.

Good policy analysis asks not only who would pay more, but how people might respond and which unintended effects could follow. Then there's the distinction between paying less tax legally and committing tax fraud.

## CHAPTER 7: The final answer
Understanding the mechanics is how we move from outrage to an informed view of the policy choices. When discussing taxes, it also helps to remember that legal rules evolve. Rates, deductions, reporting requirements, and the treatment of different assets can change through legislation and court decisions.
